The lamp at the far end of the gallery turned itself up.

Phillip was standing exactly where Nessa had left him, alone on the boards with the stone in his open hand and the smell of the mountain in every crease of his skin, and the light came up the way lamplight does not — smoothly, evenly, with no hand anywhere near the wheel. His shadow went long and thin across the gallery, and stopped.

There was a man under the lamp, broad and unhurried, in the grey of the Hall, with hot dust and lamp oil coming off him, the smell of a man who works below and has stopped noticing. He had one hand out, palm up, the way you hold it out to a horse.

"Give it here," said Hew Marrable.

The scruple was already up in the light on Phillip's palm, which was the worst possible place in the world for it. Three in the morning, boots full of warm grit, ink to the elbow, and a lie about the coal cellar half-built in his mouth, and none of it survived that open palm.

"It's a stone," he said.

"It's Ada Tull's kitchen." Marrable did not raise his voice. "You've had thirty seconds of it. Flour on her hands and a pan burnt black. She lives on Basin Row, second door with the boot-scraper. She's sixty-one and she can't remember her own bread."

Down the gallery the lamps ticked as they cooled.

"Right," Phillip said, a beat too late to be casual. "So I'll post it back."

"Aye, well." Marrable let the hand drop, and did not put it away, and did not look away either.

Two flights down, a pair of boots stopped on the stone.

Phillip heard them stop. He heard them stand a moment on the landing, in the dark, doing whatever arithmetic it is that people do at three in the morning at the bottom of a stair. Then they came back up — fast at first, then slower, the way you climb when you have already lost the argument you are having with yourself. Nessa came out of the stairhead with her jaw set and her own handprint still on her cheek and her sleeves black to the elbow.

"I got as far as the second landing," she said, to nobody, breathing hard. "Then I heard a man's voice up here. My feet turned round on their own and I want it minuted that I'd rather they hadn't."

"Miss Bellhook," said Marrable, without the slightest surprise, which was somehow worse than being caught.

He looked at the two of them a long moment — filthy, grit-crusted, small. "Keep it," he said. "You'll do a stupider thing with it if I take it off you. Come and see where it lives now."

Nessa said, "No."

She said it fast, the way she said everything, and she meant it, and Phillip heard the exact moment she stopped meaning it, about a second and a half later. Nobody made her come. That was the thing she would think about afterward, on the bad nights: nobody made her.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. Lead."

The Sump stair went down in a spiral so tight Phillip's shoulder ached from brushing the wall. The stone changed as they went — chalk, then limestone, then a black slate that sweated cold — and then, four turns down, it went dry and warm, and the air changed with it. Bread oven gone out. The Sill breath, but not the thin ghost of it that came up through the dormitory boards on winter nights. Down here it was the whole loaf, and it made the back of your neck feel looked after, which was obscene.

The iron door at the bottom had a lock the size of a Bible.

"Nine galleries," Marrable said, turning the key. "You'll walk where I walk, you'll touch what I say you can touch, and you'll not run. Nothing down here has ever hurt a soul that came honest. It's the running that gets people hurt. Ease up, both of you. Ease up."

Then he swung the door, and Phillip stopped understanding things for a while.

Gallery One was the size of the chapel at Cold Gantry and it was full. The room was not a room with a thing in it. The room was a skin. What Phillip had taken for the far wall lifted, very slightly, and settled, and lifted again, and he understood he was looking at a flank.

There were no scales. He had been promised scales by every barge-side story he had ever heard. The hide was compacted slate and pressed paper, layered like the cliff above the locks, grey going to a bruised brown where the lamp caught it, scored all over with hairline seams, thousands of them, fine as the crack in a plate. In the seams was newer matter, pale and dry, packed the way grit packs into a rope. Ash. Lamp-black. Ledger paper turned to felt. A tooth. A curl of hair. The stuff a school throws out.

It was warm as a loaf. He could feel it on his face from four yards away.

Along the upper edge, where a head would be if any of it made sense, there was an arc of eyes. Seven. They were the only wet part of her, and they opened and shut out of step with one another, slow as a tide. Phillip made the mistake of looking into two at once. They did not hold the same reflection. One had the lamp in it. One had a sash window with rain on it, and there was no window anywhere under Barrow Sill.

"That's One," said Marrable. "She's the plain one. Nothing under her but small change — market days, arguments about fences, forty years of somebody's toothache. Say good evening to her. Don't say it loud."

"Good evening," said Phillip, in the voice you use in a church.

Nessa said nothing at all.

They walked the galleries, and Phillip never once got used to it. Two lay curled with her seams packed so tight they shone. Three was restless, her breathing uneven, and Marrable stood a long while with his palm flat on her hide before he said, "She's off her feed. Third night running. They've been running the Spendhouse heavy and it's coming down faster than she can pack it." Seven made the sound they had heard through the dormitory floor two nights ago — a great slow book being turned — and hearing it at the source, Phillip found his hands shaking and could not get them to stop.

"Mind her seams," Marrable said, steering him back by the shoulder. "Don't put your fingers where the new matter's packing. It's not spite. She doesn't know you're there any more than the canal knows your boot."

"What are they?" Nessa said. First words in an hour, clipped short.

"You know what they are, girl."

"I want you to say it."

"Everything spent goes down," Marrable said. "That's the rule they don't put on the wall. You pay a coin to lift a bit of iron, and the coin doesn't stop existing. It silts. It comes down the Sluice with every coin this Hall has taken in eighty years, and where it settles thick enough it packs, and where it packs hard enough it—" he turned his hand over, and gave up on a better word. "—it sets. Like ice on a rope. And then one day it's warm and it's breathing, and the school writes it in the book as a liability and audits it every spring."

"That's a slack rope," Nessa said. "That's not an answer."

"It's the only one there is."

Then he took them to see the partition.

It was a brick wall across a dead corridor off Gallery Six, condemned that spring, waiting on masons who would not come until the thaw. Marrable set them fifteen yards back, which turned out to be twelve yards too few, and went and stood at the flank of Six with both hands on her, saying something low that Phillip could not hear.

Six breathed.

There was no fire. Phillip had known there would be no fire and had not quite believed it until now. No light, no roar, no scorch, no sound at all — just a movement of warm dry air down the corridor, the sort of draught you get when somebody opens a door two rooms away.

The wall stopped being that wall.

That was the only way he could ever describe it afterward, and it never got better. A second before, it had been a particular wall: nine courses of red brick, a bloom of damp shaped like a dog's head, a black smear where a lamp had stood for twenty years. Then those things were simply not in it. It was wall. It was the idea of wall, with nothing to say for itself, and having lost every particular of what it was, it lost the one about holding a roof up, and it came down — not blown, not shattered. It slumped. It went down like a coat off a hook, in a slow grey sigh, and the dust that came up out of it smelled of nothing at all.

The tail of the draught reached them.

It caught the loose edge of Nessa's sleeve and stirred it, and Phillip got a fistful of her collar and hauled her back three steps so hard she went over on one heel — and behind them Hew Marrable was saying, "Ease up, ease up, girl, that's it, that's my old lass," and he was saying it to the dragon. Both hands still on the hide. Not one glance down the corridor at the children.

Nessa stood up. She looked at her cuff a long moment, checking it the way you check for a splinter. Then she looked at the iron door they had come through, and at the stair beyond it going up into the ordinary cold world of grey wool and oats and examinations, and Phillip watched something in his oldest friend make up its mind and set, quietly, forever.

"I want to be on the other side of that door," she said.

"Ness—"

"I'm not arguing with you, Vane. I'm telling you where I'm standing."

Gallery Nine was at the bottom, and it was the smallest.

The room was low and narrow, a cell beside the halls above, and it was full to the roof with her. Whatever was in Gallery Nine had been folded to fit. The lamplight went out to arm's length and stopped, and beyond it there was warmth, and the smell of an oven, and a shifting that was not sound.

"Her ladyship," said Marrable, and took his hat off, which was the only truly strange thing Phillip saw him do all night.

The eyes opened.

Eleven of them. They came on in no order, scattered up the dark like windows in a street at dusk, each with its own reflection: a lock gate, a rope, a girl in a doorway, weather. Phillip stood very still and polite, because that was what he did under pressure, and he thought: they are all looking at different things.

And then, one after another, unhurried, every single one of them came round to him.

Beside him he heard Marrable stop breathing.

"That's not—" the man said. "Thirty year, lad. I have never—"

It did not speak. Later he would understand there had been nothing there yet to speak with. What it did was hand him something, the way you hand a person a cup.

A smell.

Bread — new, warm, the crust ticking as it cooled. Wet wool drying too near a stove. Tallow, the cheap kind that gutters. And green. Not a green thing; just green, a particular green with nowhere to sit, the green of a door somewhere that he had no door to attach it to — and he groped for it and found smooth blank fact where the door should have been, and it slid off him.

And under all of it, from another room, a woman's voice saying his name. Not Vane. Not lad. Just Phillip, said the way you say it to something that is not going to answer, because it is three years old and busy.

"I don't know this kitchen," Phillip said. His voice came out wrong. "I've never — I've never smelled this in my life."

Then he noticed his face was wet, and that it had been for a while.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The eyes stayed on him, patient as cattle, and the warm dry air came and went, and Hew Marrable stood with his hat in his hands and watched the boy instead of the dragon, which was new for him, and which Phillip would have reason to remember.

The climb up was two hundred and some steps and neither of them counted aloud.

At the stairhead Marrable set the lamp on the ledge and worked the key, and the iron door came to with a sound like a full stop. Nessa was already two steps up, facing the dark of the stair, not looking back. Phillip stood with his palms turned over. They were still warm. Not lamp-warm. Loaf-warm, and it would not go out of them for an hour.

"They're not beasts, lad," Marrable said, to the door rather than to him. "That's what the Charter calls them, and I've printed otherwise for thirty year and nobody with a title has ever acted on it. They're people. Filed." He turned the key the second time. "Somebody with a title needs to stand up in daylight and say that out loud one day, and it won't be me, because I'm the man they pay to keep them quiet, and I've kept them quiet, and there it is."

"She gave me something," Phillip said. "She didn't want anything for it."

Marrable stopped, hand still on the key.

"Aye," he said. "No. Nothing down there does that."

He pocketed the key, picked up the lamp, and the light swung, and for a moment all three of their shadows went up the stair together and then came apart.

"Don't come back down," said Hew Marrable. "And if you do — bring her something. She doesn't take. She only accepts."
