# The Ledger of Ninefold

*A seven-session arc. High fantasy. Rating ceiling PG-13.*

Written for Phillip, age 15. Each session is one sitting read aloud, roughly 13 minutes.

## Session 1 — The Weight of a Day

The pig-iron came onto the Tally Floor at first light on a barrow with a bad wheel, and all three hundred of them heard it coming before they saw it. A hundredweight, cast in one dull block the colour of a wet road, and when the porters tipped it onto the boards the floor took the weight the way a boat takes a wave. Dust jumped. Somebody's inkwell walked half an inch to the left and stopped.

It was the coldest morning of the year so far. The Tally Floor was grey stone with a roof too high to heat, and above the great doors the school's motto was cut a foot deep into the lintel: DEBTS ARE PAID BY THOSE WHO OWE THEM. Rain moved across the high windows in long sheets, so the light came in already used. It smelled of chalk dust and iron-gall ink, sharp as a coin held too long in a hand, and beeswax, and under all of it, faint and dry and warm, the smell the Hall never quite lost: a bread oven that had gone out some time in the night. The Sill breath. Nobody at Dragonhall mentioned it, in the way nobody mentions their own house.

Three hundred students sat in grey wool and fingerless gloves, writing out their working, and the sound of it was sleet. Three hundred nibs, none of them in step. Phillip Vane had loved that sound since his first week and had never told a living soul, because there is no version of saying it out loud that does not make you sound like an idiot.

Nessa Bellhook sat on his left with her boots hooked round the bench legs and her hands flat on the desk, which was what she did instead of fidgeting.

"Hundredweight," Nessa said. "Off the boards. Count of sixty. That's a slack rope, Vane. Sixty's long."

"Sixty's honest," Phillip said. "They priced it fair. They usually do." He heard how that sounded, one beat too late, and added, "Which is the worst thing about them."

Nessa did not laugh, which told him everything about how her morning was going.

They called him first. Alphabetical was Bellhook before Vane, but the invigilating Auditor had a list of her own, and Auditor Crake worked from it the way a woman works from a shopping list — quickly, without joy, ticking. She had a face like a closed drawer.

"Vane," she said. "Bond thirty-three. Chalk your sigil and pay."

He knelt on the boards. The chalk was cold. He drew the tally-sigil the way you are taught in Tally year — the ring, the crossbar, the little hook at the end that closes the account — and set his palm flat inside it, and looked at the iron, and began.

"The winch handle at Gantry Lock Four," Phillip said, "in November, before the sun's up."

The nibs stopped. Three hundred of them, all at once. That was the part nobody warned you about in first year: the Tally Floor goes silent when somebody spends, because everybody wants to hear what a thing is worth.

"It's iron and it's been out in the weather since my grandfather, so it's got a skin of rust on it like felt, and under the felt it's smooth where four generations of hands have worn it. It's colder than the air. That's the thing about it. You come out of a warm kitchen into a cold yard and you think you know what cold is, and then you take that handle bare-handed and it takes the heat out of you like it's owed it. There's a nick on the underside of the grip that catches the pad of your thumb every fourth turn. Not enough to cut. Enough that you count by it. And it squeals on the second turn and only the second turn, every time, for eleven years, and my father says he'll grease it and he never does, because it's how he knows the gate's moving."

He heard his own voice go quiet at the end, because it always did.

"Cold iron. Rust like felt. The nick under my thumb. Every fourth turn."

And on the last word it went.

He kept the fact. He would know for the rest of his life that there had been a winch handle at Lock Four and that it was cold. He simply would not have it any more. It was a room with the furniture carried out.

The iron rose off the boards. It came up smooth, four inches, and hung in the grey light with rain-shadow crawling over it, and Auditor Crake began to count aloud, and Phillip knelt with his palm in the chalk and let the weight settle onto his chest, because that is where it settles. At sixty she said stop, and he let go, and the floor took the wave again.

The applause was polite. He was good at this. It was the one thing he was reliably good at, and it had cost him a cold handle at dawn, and he was already doing the arithmetic on what he had left.

Then they called Nessa.

She knelt where he had knelt and drew her sigil clean — rope hands, better than his, a girl who had worked her family's lock since she was seven. She put her palm down and said, in that fast clipped way of hers that stops as soon as the point is made:

"A wet Tuesday. Autumn. There was a bell going somewhere down the valley." A pause. "I think it was the foundry. It might have been the chapel."

Phillip felt his stomach drop before the iron did.

Because that is the whole cruelty of Reckoning: vagueness is worthless. A blurred memory buys nothing. The clearer a thing is, the more you love it, the more it purchases — which means the price of every door you want opened is the best thing you own. Nessa Bellhook had decided, somewhere in the last two years, that she would not spend anything she loved. Phillip had watched her decide it. He had thought it was strength.

The iron came up perhaps two inches, tilted, and slid.

Four seconds. He would keep those four seconds for the rest of his life, and he would keep them free, because nobody ever charges you for the bad ones.

One: the block going over at the corner, slow, the way a thing goes when it has decided.

Two: a Tally girl called Bettany Rowe crossing the floor behind the invigilator's table with an armful of chalk boxes, eleven years old, looking at the chalk and not at the room, because she had been told to hurry.

Three: Nessa's face, which did not move at all.

Four: Phillip's palm already flat on the boards outside his own sigil, and his mouth already open, and no time in the world to pick something cheap.

So he did not pick something cheap.

"The door of the lock-house at Gantry Lock Four is green," he said. "It's green like the water is green when the barges have stirred the bottom up. It was painted the summer I was six and my father did it badly, and there's a run in the paint by the latch where it went too thick and dried in a drip like a tear, and I used to put my thumbnail in it. The bottom two inches are bare wood because the canal comes up over the sill in March. And my father is standing in it. He fills it. He has to duck coming out and he never remembers to, so there's a shine on the lintel where his head's been. He's got the lamp behind him so I can't see his face, only the shape of him, and he's saying get that rope in, lad, and he's holding the door open with his boot because his hands are full, and the green of it is the first colour I ever knew the name of."

He heard his voice fold in the middle. He kept going, because you have to finish.

"Green. The drip by the latch. My father in the doorway with the light behind him."

Gone.

The iron stopped falling. It did not slow; it simply stopped, eight inches above the boards and eleven inches from Bettany Rowe's shoulder, and hung there like something remembering its manners. Phillip held it for the full count of sixty, because Auditor Crake had started counting and it did not occur to him to argue. Bettany walked out from under it, understood what had happened only when she was clear of it, and sat down on the floor.

The Tally Floor came apart. Three hundred students on their feet, benches going over, a noise like weather. Auditor Crake wrote one word on her list, and Phillip, letting the block down onto the boards, was close enough to read it upside down. Distinguished.

He was glowing. That was the honest word for it. It went through him warm and complete, and it did not last past the moment he tried to picture the door.

He knew the door was there. He knew he had a father and that his father stood in it. He reached for the colour and got the word green, which is a fact, and behind the word there was a doorway he could not see, with a shape in it that had no face.

Nessa got up off her knees. She had passed. Her name was on the list with his.

"Nessa —"

"Don't." She was already gathering her chalk. Her voice had gone flat and consonant-heavy. "Don't gantry me, Vane."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were." She looked at him then, properly, and whatever was in her face was not gratitude and was not anger and had no name he could find. "You'll want thanking. And I can't." A beat. "I don't know why. But I can't."

She went. He stood on the Tally Floor with three hundred people saying his name and could not think of a single thing to do with his hands.

The gallery ran the length of the hall, twelve feet up, and staff watched the Assize from it the way owners watch horses. Bursar Ilsabet Quorn stood at the rail with her gloves folded over one wrist — grey silk, spectacles on a chain, and a stillness that made the students below look like weather. She waited until the noise thinned, and then said, not loudly, in a voice like slate under cold water:

"Mr Vane."

The hall found quiet for her without being asked.

"Bond thirty-three," said Ilsabet Quorn. "The task was priced at one particular of ordinary weight. I have some experience of pricing. What you laid down was worth four times that, and you laid it down in under four seconds, without instruction, for a girl who had not asked you." She took a small black book from her sleeve, and a pencil, and wrote in it. It took her perhaps five words. "I have noted it."

"Thank you, Bursar," Phillip said, because his sentences went short and polite under pressure.

"It is not praise, Mr Vane. It is a record." She closed the book. "Talent is common in this valley. Willingness is not, and it appears on no examination we set." She put the book away. "Go and eat. You will be colder than you think."

She was right about that, and he did not think about the book again for a very long time.

The Counting Bell rang long that day — one stroke for every hundred coins the school laid out — and by evening the students had stopped counting and started listening to it the way you listen to rain.

That night was the coldest night of the year. The fourth-floor dormitory had eleven beds and one window that did not shut, and the boys slept in their stockings and their coats. Phillip lay awake and listened to the ten o'clock bell go past ten, and past twelve, and past fifteen, and stop at a number nobody in the room said out loud.

Then the warmth came up through the boards. It did that on still nights — the Sill breath, dry and dusty and faintly sweet, an oven cooling somewhere a very long way down. And under it, so low it was more floor than sound, came a noise like a great slow book being turned. One enormous page, lifting and settling.

Phillip got out of bed. The boards were freezing under his bare feet, and he stood in the dark between the beds with everyone else asleep, and he was thinking about a doorway he could not see, and before he could stop himself he said, out loud, to nobody:

"It was green. I know it was green."

The book turned again.

It came after he spoke. Not with him, not before. After, the way an answer comes after a question, and nearer than the first time, and the boards under his bare feet on the coldest night of the year were warm.

## Session 2 — Down the Sluice

He did not sleep. Somewhere in the small hours that stopped being a misery and turned into a plan. Phillip Vane sat on the boards of the fourth-floor dormitory with his bare feet on the one warm plank, listening to fifteen other Quires breathe, waiting for enough grey to work by. Rain on the slate above him, steady as a clerk. Everything else in the Hall was cold. That was the whole of his reasoning.

He prised the plank up with a spoon handle he had been thinning against a step for two days. The nails came out with a sound like small teeth letting go. Underneath was a gap a hand deep, floored with grit, and the grit was dry. Nothing in that mountain was dry. He put two fingers in it and it was warm as a cat's back, and when he drew them into the window light every crease of his knuckles was lit with points of glitter, silver and black and one almost green. It smelled like a bread oven gone out — the whole school's smell, concentrated, the way a smell is when you have found the thing making it.

Nessa Bellhook woke the way she did everything, all at once and unimpressed.

"That's a floor, Vane."

"It's warm."

"It's a floor. Put it back." She looked at the glitter on his hand, and something went across her face and was filed. "Wash that off."

At second bell they had Practicals with Auditor Skeat, a narrow man with ink to the second knuckle who taught the Quire year its sigils and did not enjoy it. Phillip drew his tally-sigil badly on purpose, to have the man's attention, and asked the honest question.

"Sir. When a coin's spent — when it's gone out of you. Where does it go?"

Chalk dust hung in the cold light. Forty nibs kept going.

"It is discharged," said Auditor Skeat.

"Yes, sir. Where to?"

"Discharged, Mr Vane, is the answer. It is not a place, it is a condition. A debt is discharged. Coal is burnt. Water goes down." He tapped the board twice. "Redraw that. Your crossbar's shy."

Phillip redrew the crossbar. What stayed with him was not the answer, which was nothing, but the forty heads that never came up. Water goes down into somebody's cellar, and then it is somebody's problem, and somebody is generally the lockkeeper.

Odalric Pell found him at the foot of the Tally Floor stair before supper, which meant he had gone looking. Sixteen, first in his year, standing too straight, like a man being measured.

"You are the one who asked about discharge," Odd said. "In the — the room with the long windows. Skeat."

"Practicals."

"Practicals. Yes." He did not blink at the gap, and Phillip understood without being told that he was not to blink at it either. "His answer was administrative. Marrable, third edition, chapter two: spent particulars are conducted by gravity to the lower workings. It is drainage, Vane. Every hall has a — a drain. Ours is called the Sluice."

"There's a grate under the fourth floor."

"There are eleven grates. The one you want is behind the lamp gallery. I have the key. I want a warming."

"On what?"

"My carrel. In the — the long room with the desks and the cold end. The ink thickens and my hand is unreliable after ten. A trivial reckoning. Chapter nine. A carrel wants a warm coin, and I find I am currently out of that particular denomination."

Phillip thought about asking what happens to a person who runs out of warm ones. He did not ask, and afterwards he thought about that a good deal.

"All right," he said.

He drew the sigil under the desk and did the thing the Hall calls spending and everyone else in Ilmarch calls the saying.

"The summer I turned twelve my father let me work Lock Four on my own," he said, and his voice did what it always did, which was slow down and get greedy. "August, nine days without rain, which never happens. The capstan iron had been in the sun since six and I sat on it to wait for the Ellenbeck coming up light. It was so hot through my trousers I kept having to shift. Tar smell off the beams. Green weed drying white on the gate. My father came out and threw me an apple and it hit me in the chest because I wasn't looking. The heat of that iron went up my back all the way to my neck. It's the warmest I have ever been in my life."

On the last word it went.

He knew, still, that he had once been warm on a capstan, the way he knew the date of the Charter. The heat was not there, and where it had been was a smooth place, like a wall where a picture has come down. The carrel sighed in its joints and began, gently, to be warm.

Odd put his hand flat on the desk and left it there.

"That was very good," he said. "You did not haggle. Most people haggle, and it is a waste of everybody's evening. You are going to do extremely well here, Vane. I mean that kindly."

Phillip said thank you, because he had been raised to, and went away with the key in his fist and a feeling he could not name and did not like.

They went down at one in the morning, after the porters' round had gone up to the roof lofts. Nessa came because Nessa was not going to let him go alone, and said so once and never mentioned it again.

Behind the lamp gallery, low in the wall, was an iron grate the size of an oven door. It breathed on them when he opened it — not cold, not damp, a slow dry exhalation with the bread-oven smell in it, and under that lamp oil and something like the inside of a purse.

The Sluice was a brick throat, pitched like a steep stair, wide enough for a boy on hands and knees, and laid by somebody who cared: every brick keyed, the joints struck neat, curving away below like a swallowed pipe. It was warm and completely dry, and it glittered the entire way down, hazed with the same silt that had been under his bed, so that when Nessa held the candle out the shaft looked like a road after a frost.

"Somebody built this." Her voice came back off the brick, small and flat. "Somebody sat down with a rule and a pen, Vane. You don't strike joints like that on a hole. They knew where it went."

The silt deepened as they went, past the ankles, over the tops of his boots, so that his feet walked in warmth. Two hundred feet down the throat widened into a shelf where the silt had drifted like sand, and in the drifts there were stones. Hundreds of them, none bigger than a thumbnail, lensed and wet-looking, holding the candlelight in their middles and giving it back changed. They looked exactly like spilled coin.

"Scruples," Phillip said. "That's what Odd meant by hoard."

He crouched. He knew the rule — it was recited at them twice a year — and took his glove off anyway.

It was not like a memory. A memory is yours and holds still. This was thirty seconds of somebody's Tuesday going through him at speed: a low kitchen, whitewashed, one window over a stone sink; hands in front of him that were a woman's, floured to the wrist, the flour gone to grey paste in the creases; a pan burnt black and standing in water; a red cloth folded on the sill; a doorway with the light of a yard beyond. And a voice — her voice, out of a throat that was not his — calling a child's name through that doorway, twice, the second time with the small rising bend at the end that means now.

He did not know the name. He knew, absolutely, that she had loved whoever it belonged to.

Then the kitchen let go and he was on his knees in warm grit with his heart going like a rope through a sheave.

Nessa had backed up a step. "You're shaking."

"There's a woman in it, Nessa. Her kitchen. She called somebody in for their tea." He held it out on his palm, which was the wrong thing to do and he did it anyway. "Take one. You have to feel it."

"No."

"It's thirty seconds—"

"I'd rather not know." Her arms were crossed, her jaw set, the candle shivering in her fist. "That's allowed, Vane. Wanting to know isn't a virtue. It's just a thing you can't stop doing."

Above them, a lamp came round the bend of the gallery and laid a yellow tongue across the grate.

They went flat. Boots on brick, unhurried. The grate at the top stood open on Odd's key with two Quires' handprints all over it.

Phillip was drawing the sigil in the silt with his finger before he had decided to.

"My twelfth birthday," he said, low and fast and beautifully, because he could not do it any other way. "My father made eel pie and got the pastry wrong and we ate it anyway. There was one candle, a stub off the lock lamp, and it went out before I could do anything with it. The kitchen was full of steam and the window ran. He gave me a knife with a horn handle wrapped in sailcloth and he didn't say happy birthday, he said, that'll want oiling. His hands were so big round the cup. And he watched me open it. He watched me the whole time I was opening it—"

The word went. Two hundred feet above, the door drew itself shut against the porter's hand and sat in its frame like a thing that had never been open. The yellow tongue went off the brick.

And Phillip knelt in the dark, and for three seconds he did not know what he was doing down there.

Not the way you forget an errand. He knew his name, and the girl breathing beside him. But the reason — the thing that had got him out of bed and down a chimney of brick at one in the morning — was not in him. It was a smooth place. He touched the warm silt and thought, quite calmly, that he had come a long way down for something.

Then it came back, all of it, his father and the four years, and he was so glad that he made a small noise.

"What," said Nessa.

"Nothing. Cramp."

It was the first lie he had ever told her, and it was easy, and that was the worst of it.

The climb took an hour. The silt got everywhere — down his collar, into both boots, warm against his ankles the whole way like something breathing on him — and brick dust and iron-gall ink went up his arms, so that when he came out onto the fourth-floor lamp gallery he was black to the elbow, and so was she, with a print of her own hand on her cheek.

He did not put the scruple back. He had told himself on the way up that he would, and every time his hand went near his pocket it went past, and by the gallery the question had quietly stopped being a question.

It was about three. The rain had stopped, and the Hall was making the noises an empty building makes as it cools. Nessa scraped her boots on a board, twice, and did not look at him.

"That's a slack rope, all of it," she said. "I've always known, and now I've seen it, and I'd rather have kept on just knowing." She rubbed at her cheek, which made it worse. "Going to bed. Wash your arms. They'll have you at bell."

"Nessa—"

"Don't gantry me, Vane. Not tonight."

She went. He listened to her feet to the stairhead and down two flights, and then there was nothing but the lamp hissing on its bracket.

He took the scruple out and held it up to the light. Smaller than he remembered. Grey-brown, lensed, with a seed of light in its middle not quite the right colour for a lamp. The flour was in there, and the red cloth, and a woman calling a name into a yard, and none of it showed. It sat in his filthy fingers, no heavier than a farthing.

Then he noticed the smell. He had been noticing it a while without noticing it, the way you do. It came off the stone warm and dry: a bread oven an hour after the bread is out and the door left open. The Sill breath.

He turned his hand over.

It was on his skin as well — all over both hands, up to the elbow, in every crease and under every nail. The smell of the mountain, warm as a loaf, come up out of the dark with him. It was not coming off.

## Session 3 — The Old Girls

The lamp at the far end of the gallery turned itself up.

Phillip was standing exactly where Nessa had left him, alone on the boards with the stone in his open hand and the smell of the mountain in every crease of his skin, and the light came up the way lamplight does not — smoothly, evenly, with no hand anywhere near the wheel. His shadow went long and thin across the gallery, and stopped.

There was a man under the lamp, broad and unhurried, in the grey of the Hall, with hot dust and lamp oil coming off him, the smell of a man who works below and has stopped noticing. He had one hand out, palm up, the way you hold it out to a horse.

"Give it here," said Hew Marrable.

The scruple was already up in the light on Phillip's palm, which was the worst possible place in the world for it. Three in the morning, boots full of warm grit, ink to the elbow, and a lie about the coal cellar half-built in his mouth, and none of it survived that open palm.

"It's a stone," he said.

"It's Ada Tull's kitchen." Marrable did not raise his voice. "You've had thirty seconds of it. Flour on her hands and a pan burnt black. She lives on Basin Row, second door with the boot-scraper. She's sixty-one and she can't remember her own bread."

Down the gallery the lamps ticked as they cooled.

"Right," Phillip said, a beat too late to be casual. "So I'll post it back."

"Aye, well." Marrable let the hand drop, and did not put it away, and did not look away either.

Two flights down, a pair of boots stopped on the stone.

Phillip heard them stop. He heard them stand a moment on the landing, in the dark, doing whatever arithmetic it is that people do at three in the morning at the bottom of a stair. Then they came back up — fast at first, then slower, the way you climb when you have already lost the argument you are having with yourself. Nessa came out of the stairhead with her jaw set and her own handprint still on her cheek and her sleeves black to the elbow.

"I got as far as the second landing," she said, to nobody, breathing hard. "Then I heard a man's voice up here. My feet turned round on their own and I want it minuted that I'd rather they hadn't."

"Miss Bellhook," said Marrable, without the slightest surprise, which was somehow worse than being caught.

He looked at the two of them a long moment — filthy, grit-crusted, small. "Keep it," he said. "You'll do a stupider thing with it if I take it off you. Come and see where it lives now."

Nessa said, "No."

She said it fast, the way she said everything, and she meant it, and Phillip heard the exact moment she stopped meaning it, about a second and a half later. Nobody made her come. That was the thing she would think about afterward, on the bad nights: nobody made her.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. Lead."

The Sump stair went down in a spiral so tight Phillip's shoulder ached from brushing the wall. The stone changed as they went — chalk, then limestone, then a black slate that sweated cold — and then, four turns down, it went dry and warm, and the air changed with it. Bread oven gone out. The Sill breath, but not the thin ghost of it that came up through the dormitory boards on winter nights. Down here it was the whole loaf, and it made the back of your neck feel looked after, which was obscene.

The iron door at the bottom had a lock the size of a Bible.

"Nine galleries," Marrable said, turning the key. "You'll walk where I walk, you'll touch what I say you can touch, and you'll not run. Nothing down here has ever hurt a soul that came honest. It's the running that gets people hurt. Ease up, both of you. Ease up."

Then he swung the door, and Phillip stopped understanding things for a while.

Gallery One was the size of the chapel at Cold Gantry and it was full. The room was not a room with a thing in it. The room was a skin. What Phillip had taken for the far wall lifted, very slightly, and settled, and lifted again, and he understood he was looking at a flank.

There were no scales. He had been promised scales by every barge-side story he had ever heard. The hide was compacted slate and pressed paper, layered like the cliff above the locks, grey going to a bruised brown where the lamp caught it, scored all over with hairline seams, thousands of them, fine as the crack in a plate. In the seams was newer matter, pale and dry, packed the way grit packs into a rope. Ash. Lamp-black. Ledger paper turned to felt. A tooth. A curl of hair. The stuff a school throws out.

It was warm as a loaf. He could feel it on his face from four yards away.

Along the upper edge, where a head would be if any of it made sense, there was an arc of eyes. Seven. They were the only wet part of her, and they opened and shut out of step with one another, slow as a tide. Phillip made the mistake of looking into two at once. They did not hold the same reflection. One had the lamp in it. One had a sash window with rain on it, and there was no window anywhere under Barrow Sill.

"That's One," said Marrable. "She's the plain one. Nothing under her but small change — market days, arguments about fences, forty years of somebody's toothache. Say good evening to her. Don't say it loud."

"Good evening," said Phillip, in the voice you use in a church.

Nessa said nothing at all.

They walked the galleries, and Phillip never once got used to it. Two lay curled with her seams packed so tight they shone. Three was restless, her breathing uneven, and Marrable stood a long while with his palm flat on her hide before he said, "She's off her feed. Third night running. They've been running the Spendhouse heavy and it's coming down faster than she can pack it." Seven made the sound they had heard through the dormitory floor two nights ago — a great slow book being turned — and hearing it at the source, Phillip found his hands shaking and could not get them to stop.

"Mind her seams," Marrable said, steering him back by the shoulder. "Don't put your fingers where the new matter's packing. It's not spite. She doesn't know you're there any more than the canal knows your boot."

"What are they?" Nessa said. First words in an hour, clipped short.

"You know what they are, girl."

"I want you to say it."

"Everything spent goes down," Marrable said. "That's the rule they don't put on the wall. You pay a coin to lift a bit of iron, and the coin doesn't stop existing. It silts. It comes down the Sluice with every coin this Hall has taken in eighty years, and where it settles thick enough it packs, and where it packs hard enough it—" he turned his hand over, and gave up on a better word. "—it sets. Like ice on a rope. And then one day it's warm and it's breathing, and the school writes it in the book as a liability and audits it every spring."

"That's a slack rope," Nessa said. "That's not an answer."

"It's the only one there is."

Then he took them to see the partition.

It was a brick wall across a dead corridor off Gallery Six, condemned that spring, waiting on masons who would not come until the thaw. Marrable set them fifteen yards back, which turned out to be twelve yards too few, and went and stood at the flank of Six with both hands on her, saying something low that Phillip could not hear.

Six breathed.

There was no fire. Phillip had known there would be no fire and had not quite believed it until now. No light, no roar, no scorch, no sound at all — just a movement of warm dry air down the corridor, the sort of draught you get when somebody opens a door two rooms away.

The wall stopped being that wall.

That was the only way he could ever describe it afterward, and it never got better. A second before, it had been a particular wall: nine courses of red brick, a bloom of damp shaped like a dog's head, a black smear where a lamp had stood for twenty years. Then those things were simply not in it. It was wall. It was the idea of wall, with nothing to say for itself, and having lost every particular of what it was, it lost the one about holding a roof up, and it came down — not blown, not shattered. It slumped. It went down like a coat off a hook, in a slow grey sigh, and the dust that came up out of it smelled of nothing at all.

The tail of the draught reached them.

It caught the loose edge of Nessa's sleeve and stirred it, and Phillip got a fistful of her collar and hauled her back three steps so hard she went over on one heel — and behind them Hew Marrable was saying, "Ease up, ease up, girl, that's it, that's my old lass," and he was saying it to the dragon. Both hands still on the hide. Not one glance down the corridor at the children.

Nessa stood up. She looked at her cuff a long moment, checking it the way you check for a splinter. Then she looked at the iron door they had come through, and at the stair beyond it going up into the ordinary cold world of grey wool and oats and examinations, and Phillip watched something in his oldest friend make up its mind and set, quietly, forever.

"I want to be on the other side of that door," she said.

"Ness—"

"I'm not arguing with you, Vane. I'm telling you where I'm standing."

Gallery Nine was at the bottom, and it was the smallest.

The room was low and narrow, a cell beside the halls above, and it was full to the roof with her. Whatever was in Gallery Nine had been folded to fit. The lamplight went out to arm's length and stopped, and beyond it there was warmth, and the smell of an oven, and a shifting that was not sound.

"Her ladyship," said Marrable, and took his hat off, which was the only truly strange thing Phillip saw him do all night.

The eyes opened.

Eleven of them. They came on in no order, scattered up the dark like windows in a street at dusk, each with its own reflection: a lock gate, a rope, a girl in a doorway, weather. Phillip stood very still and polite, because that was what he did under pressure, and he thought: they are all looking at different things.

And then, one after another, unhurried, every single one of them came round to him.

Beside him he heard Marrable stop breathing.

"That's not—" the man said. "Thirty year, lad. I have never—"

It did not speak. Later he would understand there had been nothing there yet to speak with. What it did was hand him something, the way you hand a person a cup.

A smell.

Bread — new, warm, the crust ticking as it cooled. Wet wool drying too near a stove. Tallow, the cheap kind that gutters. And green. Not a green thing; just green, a particular green with nowhere to sit, the green of a door somewhere that he had no door to attach it to — and he groped for it and found smooth blank fact where the door should have been, and it slid off him.

And under all of it, from another room, a woman's voice saying his name. Not Vane. Not lad. Just Phillip, said the way you say it to something that is not going to answer, because it is three years old and busy.

"I don't know this kitchen," Phillip said. His voice came out wrong. "I've never — I've never smelled this in my life."

Then he noticed his face was wet, and that it had been for a while.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The eyes stayed on him, patient as cattle, and the warm dry air came and went, and Hew Marrable stood with his hat in his hands and watched the boy instead of the dragon, which was new for him, and which Phillip would have reason to remember.

The climb up was two hundred and some steps and neither of them counted aloud.

At the stairhead Marrable set the lamp on the ledge and worked the key, and the iron door came to with a sound like a full stop. Nessa was already two steps up, facing the dark of the stair, not looking back. Phillip stood with his palms turned over. They were still warm. Not lamp-warm. Loaf-warm, and it would not go out of them for an hour.

"They're not beasts, lad," Marrable said, to the door rather than to him. "That's what the Charter calls them, and I've printed otherwise for thirty year and nobody with a title has ever acted on it. They're people. Filed." He turned the key the second time. "Somebody with a title needs to stand up in daylight and say that out loud one day, and it won't be me, because I'm the man they pay to keep them quiet, and I've kept them quiet, and there it is."

"She gave me something," Phillip said. "She didn't want anything for it."

Marrable stopped, hand still on the key.

"Aye," he said. "No. Nothing down there does that."

He pocketed the key, picked up the lamp, and the light swung, and for a moment all three of their shadows went up the stair together and then came apart.

"Don't come back down," said Hew Marrable. "And if you do — bring her something. She doesn't take. She only accepts."

## Session 4 — A Gift Freely Given

The iron door at the head of the Sump stair had a keyhole the size of a thumbnail and, behind it, nothing at all. Hew Marrable had not simply locked it and pocketed the key; he had taken the wards out of it, the way you take the shoes off a horse you don't mean to ride again.

Phillip stood at it twice that week, rain coming down the passage wall in slow brown seams, the Sill breath coming up round the frame regardless — warm, dry, bread oven gone out. He put his palm flat on it. Cold on the surface; under the cold, not cold. A week ago his hands had held that warmth in the hide of a living thing with eleven eyes.

He asked Nessa on the Thursday, in daylight, because night made things sound possible that were not. They were in the wash-yard behind the refectory, rain ticking on the slates, and he told it flat, the way she liked things told.

"There's the other way down," he said. "The Sluice. That grate under the boards is still slack. I'd go tonight."

Nessa wrung out a cloth. She did it properly, hands like rope. "No."

"You haven't heard the rest."

"Don't need it. That's a slack rope, Vane, and I'm not standing under it. I watched a wall stop being a wall. It went quiet and forgot how to hold a roof up, and it wasn't even angry."

"I'm not asking you to touch anything."

"You're asking me to be stood there when you do." Her voice went short, the way it did when the point had been made. "I want the post. I want my mother off the water. There's one road to that and it runs through this Hall."

"Right," he said, and it took him some doing.

"You could just not go."

"I could," he agreed, one beat too late for it to be a joke. She heard the lateness. Something crossed her face and she filed it at once, in the place where she filed things. It was very tidy in there.

He went at half past midnight and did not light his candle. The Sluice glittered.

It was a brick throat, warm to the hand, every seam silted with the fine dry grit that comes off a school making its money out of children. It got in his cuffs and his teeth, and here and there something in the drift threw the light back like coin at the bottom of a canal. Ada Tull's scruple was still in his pocket, long enough now to have gone the temperature of him.

Two hundred feet of it, down on his heels with one hand on the brick. Then the throat opened and set him on the floor of the Sumps, and the air changed the way it changes in a barn where something very large is asleep.

He needed no lamp in Gallery Nine. Ninefold's seams gave off their own slow working glow, hairline cracks all down the flank, so that the whole slope of her looked like a hillside at dusk with the lights of a town coming on in it.

Eleven eyes opened. Not together — in a travelling wave, first to last, like locks filling down a flight.

Phillip's mouth went dry as chalk. What he had rehearsed for six days came out smaller than it had in his head.

"I've brought you something," he said. "I'm not paying with it. I want that clear, because I don't know how this is done and I'd rather be honest than smooth. Nobody else on earth has this. Not my father. It's mine, and I'm giving it to you, and I'm not asking for it back."

He did not draw the tally-sigil. His hand went to the chalk out of two years of habit and he made himself set it down on the stone, and the not-drawing felt like standing at a lock gate with the winch in both hands and refusing to turn it.

Then he said it the way he always said his coins, in far more detail than anything required, because saying them was the last time he had them.

"It was the morning after he signed. I was eleven. It had frosted in the night and the basin had gone white, and the water was that flat black it goes when it's colder than the air. I came out for the coal and he was sat on the beam of the lower gate with his back to the house. He had his good coat on over his nightshirt, which he never did, and his boots not laced. There was a paper in his hand, folded in three, one corner of it going soft where his thumb had been. His shoulders were going. No noise at all — he was crying the way men cry when they've worked out somebody might hear, all held up in the top of the chest, his breath coming out white in little bits. The frost on the beam had gone to wet under him. There was a moorhen out on the black and it went across and he watched it the whole way. I stood in the doorway with the coal scuttle and counted to about twelve. Then I went back inside and put the scuttle down quiet and I never told him. I've never told anybody."

He stopped, and waited for the last word to take it, because that is what last words do.

It did not go.

The moorhen was still there. So was the soft corner of the paper. He tried it again the way you press a tooth to see whether it hurts, and all of it came — the white breath, the unlaced boots — whole, and his. He had given away the best thing he owned and he still had it. Nobody had ever mentioned that this was a way things could go.

Ninefold shifted — not a movement so much as a settling, a slow adjustment of great weight, like snow going the wrong way. Then she spoke, in pieces, and every piece belonged to somebody else.

"Down," said Ninefold, in a bargeman's flat wide vowels, "and down, and it does not" — and the voice handed itself over mid-word to an old woman's, thin and north-country and amused about something long finished — "come up, love. It never comes up."

The clauses kept changing hands. A clerk's careful diction. A girl laughing at the start of a sentence and not the end of it.

"We are what the school throws away," said Ninefold, in four voices, and one of them was a small boy's.

The gallery went quiet around that one. Not silent — quiet; the particular quiet of a room where everybody has decided at once not to look at one thing. Phillip's chest hurt. He put it down to the climb.

Then he asked what he had come two hundred feet down to ask, and his sentences went short and polite, the way they did when it mattered.

"My father sold four years. His four years of me, from the start to when I was four. Tobin Vane, Gantry Lock Four. I think they came down here. I'd like them back, please, and I'll pay what it costs. I've better coin than anyone in my year and I'll spend all of it, and if that's not enough I'll find more. Name the price."

The eyes did not blink. When Ninefold answered, the chorus came slower.

"They are here," she said, in the clerk. "In order. Entire."

"Not stolen," said the old woman.

"Not taken by any monster," said a man he had never met, with a cough at the end of it. "Filed. By your school. Signed for, carried down, discharged, and the paper kept."

"Then I'll buy them off you," Phillip said. "Anything. Say it."

And here the chorus did something worse than roar. It went gentle.

"Child," said Ninefold, in a woman's voice with flour in it — and his hand went to his pocket without his permission — "nothing bought ever comes back. Not to you. Not to him. Not to me. It is the one law your Hall has never had to enforce, because it enforces itself." A pause; a shifting of enormous warm weight. "You cannot pay me. I am made of paying."

"Then there's nothing."

"There is one thing." All the borrowed voices arrived on it together and did not agree on the accent, so that it came from every direction at once, low and dry as pages turning. "I can be read."

He stood with the chalk by his boot and his whole plan dead on the floor in front of him. Two years of spending, every distinguished written in a margin, all of it built on a strongbox he had only to earn the key to. There was no strongbox. There was no thief. There was a mouth at the top of the mountain that had been eating for eighty years, and there was this, patient and warm and full, waiting to be said aloud.

He came out on the fourth floor a little after four, grit in his cuffs, the world rearranging itself the whole way up. The dormitory was black and cold and smelled of wet wool. He walked in, stopped between the two rows, and could not think which bed was his.

Not forgotten. Worse. He knew the fact of it perfectly — sixteen beds, his on the side away from the window — but the fact sat in his head like a note in somebody else's handwriting, and the particular was gone. He turned round twice in the dark, very calmly, calm being the only thing left he could afford.

"Fourth from the arch," said Odd, from the doorway.

Phillip did not move.

Odalric Pell came down the row in his dressing gown, hair perfectly flat, counting under his breath, touching each bed-end with two fingers. He stopped and turned the blanket back.

"This one. The one with the marking on the — " a tiny silence, exactly where a noun should have been, "— on the thing at the end. Your number's on it. Get in."

Phillip got in. His hands had begun shaking on the last flight and had not stopped.

"You were in the plumbing," said Odd.

"Yes."

"I am not interested in why." Odd sat down two beds over, very upright, hands on knees. "I require you for the Martinmas practical. Marrable, third edition, chapter nine, is quite clear that it takes two willing, and there is nobody else in this building with your — " the silence again, longer, "— your appetite. So you will be well on the third of next month. That is the arrangement."

"Odd —"

"That is the arrangement," Odd said again, in exactly the same tone, which was how he ended conversations.

The dawn bell went at six, and an Auditor came down the row with a lamp and a clipboard, asking each boy his own name and the day of the week. They did not do that every morning, and every boy knew what it was for.

"Pell. Vane," said the Auditor. "Either of you out of this room in the night?"

"No, sir," said Odd, without a pause in it. "Vane was in his bed at the eleven bell. I noticed because he snores like the thing on the canal. The boat thing."

The Auditor wrote something and moved on. Neither boy said the word for it, then or ever. Odd lay down facing the wall and was asleep inside four minutes, and Phillip understood that Odalric Pell had counted the beds for him because Odalric Pell had once had to count them for himself, in the dark, and had never told a soul either.

Grey came up the valley. Rain on slate. Barge horns at the basin, two long and one short. Phillip took out the sheet he had written at two that morning. Dad — I'm all right. Don't sign anything else. I know where the years went and I'm going to get them. There it stopped. The Bursar's office read the letters for the school's protection, and nothing after that sentence was true any more. He folded it in three, which he did not notice himself doing, and pushed it flat under the mattress.

Then he lay on the bed that was his, on the fact of it, and tried to see the door of the lock-house at Gantry Lock Four. His father had painted it the summer before the signing and gone on about the price of the paint for a week. He knew the fact of the colour the way he knew the fact of the bed.

Green, or blue. It would not come.

And underneath that, still turning, still warm, the last thing she had said as he stepped back toward the Sluice — not the bargeman, not the old woman, not the clerk, but that small boy's voice again, which he would not understand for years.

"Read me, and I will say your name the way it was said."

## Session 5 — The Spendhouse Ledger

The dawn bell had stopped a good while before Phillip stopped hearing it. He lay on his back with his boots on and ink to the elbow, listening to rain find its way along the slates. Two beds over, Odd Pell slept precisely, arms folded like a man laid out for inspection. Under the mattress was a letter home he could not send, because the Bursar's office read letters for the school's protection.

He was trying to picture the door of the lock-house at Gantry Lock Four. He knew it was painted. Green, he thought, and there was no picture behind the word, and it might as well have been blue.

Nessa Bellhook came down the row of beds without pretending she was looking for anything else.

"You went down again," she said.

"Morning," said Phillip.

"Don't." She stopped at the foot of the bed, hair still wet from the yard pump. "You've grit in your boot seams and you smell like a bread oven gone out. Don't gantry me, Vane."

Phillip put his joke in one beat too late. "You're up early."

"I've news." She sat on the frame. Then, flat and fast: "Auditor's assistantship. Second year. It isn't done before Reckoner. Quorn's office found a precedent." She breathed out. "Wages, Vane. My mother's chest goes bad in November and there's a bed in Ilmarch costs eleven shillings."

The Sill breath came up warm and dry through the boards, an oven the morning after.

"They gave it you three weeks after you watched that wall come down," Phillip said.

"They gave it me because I'm good."

"Three weeks after."

"Yes," said Nessa. "Both."

That was the whole of Nessa Bellhook: perfect eyesight, and a decision not to move.

So he told her. Where the money came from. That his father's four years were down in the dark with everyone else's, and that whoever had arranged it would have written it down, because everything in Ilmarch is written down twice.

Nessa heard him out. "I'll get you to the road. I know the lock path and the porter's round." She held up a finger. "I'll walk you to the Spendhouse gate. I won't go through it. Not the gate, not the yard, not the step. That's my limit, and I'm saying it out loud so you can't tell yourself later I never did."

"Nessa —"

"Out loud, Vane." Her chin came up. "So it's mine."

It was the most honest thing anybody said to him all that term.

They went at half eleven, under rain gone so fine it was more like breathing than weather. The lock cut smelled of cold green water and coal ash, and barge horns sounded from the basin, two long and one short. The Spendhouse stood behind a low wall: three storeys of grey stone with the gutters kept clean, which in Ilmarch is how you know a building is loved. Gaslight in the fanlight, and no sign on it at all.

Nessa stopped at the gatepost with her hands in her sleeves. "There," she said, and did not move another inch.

Inside, it was a bank in miniature, and somebody had spent real money making it feel like nothing whatever. A dark counter, a brass grille, twelve chairs with green baize seats set in two rows facing each other, so that nobody has anywhere to look. A clock with a slow brass tick. Over it all the smell of iron-gall ink, sharp as a coin held too long in a hand, and beeswax, and under that a faint sweetness, like a tin that once held sugar.

He was beneath the stairs in the dark when the inner door opened and a woman came out: well dressed, a fur collar beaded with rain, and nothing at all wrong with her. She fastened her glove without looking at it and wished the clerk a good evening in the voice of any woman leaving any office.

Out on the step her driver got down, a grey careful man, and took his hat off.

"The blue one, Mrs Corrant," he said gently. "Blue body, black wheels, lamp bracket bent on the near side. That one's yours. I'm Rennick, ma'am. I've driven for you eleven years."

"Of course," said Mrs Corrant pleasantly. "Of course it is."

She got in, he shut the door, the wheels went off down the wet road, and nothing had happened. That was the dread of it: a man who had said that sentence many times before, and had learned to say it kindly.

The ledger room was one flight up. The stair had a strip of carpet down the middle of it and smelled of cold brass and beeswax, and the door at the top had a lock in it the size of a hymn book.

Phillip knelt on the boards in the dark and drew the tally-sigil with the stub of chalk from his cuff. Then he said his coin out loud, all of it, more than the work wanted, because saying it was the last time he would have it.

"There's a lamp room under the stair at Lock Four," he said. "It's the size of a cupboard and it's got one high window with wire in the glass, and the can of oil lives on the shelf by the door with a dent in its shoulder where my grandmother dropped it. Every evening of my life my father filled the storm lamp in there and never once got a drop on his hands. You had to hold the funnel and count to eight. And the smell of it when the can got low and started to gulp — that thin, hot, peppery smell that got into your jumper and came out again in bed — that's what I'm paying you."

The lock gave with a small sound, like a tooth coming out of a comb. Phillip stayed on his knees a moment. He knew, as a fact, that lamp oil has a smell, and that he had once been able to name it across a room. He could not have told a living soul what it was.

He wiped the chalk off with his sleeve and went in.

Eighty years of ledgers, cold iron and paper. The bond scholarships were kept apart from the oxblood Quietage books, in a slimmer volume, because they were a different sort of money.

Forty-one entries.

He had come expecting one wicked man's name at the bottom of this. What he found was columns — and before the columns, an index.

The first leaf of the slim volume was not an entry at all. It was a schedule of them, ruled with a straight edge in a fine round hand: forty-one lines, one line to a bond, and each line carrying a number, a name, a place in the sequence, and the date the sequence was taken. Nothing else. No sums, no signatures. It was the sort of page a man makes so he never has to hunt through his own book again, and it sat at the front of the volume like a table of contents in a novel.

The sequence ran unbroken. One to forty-one, in order, with dates, over eleven years.

Phillip put his thumb down the list until he found thirty-three, and Vane beside it, and the schedule sent him on to the ninth page, where the entries themselves ran four to a page with generous margins, and he turned the leaves and read.

VANE, TOBIN. Lockkeeper. Gantry Lock Four, Threnmoor.

Sold: the subject's first four years, entire and in sequence.

Credit applied: tuition and maintenance, Charterhouse of Reckoning at Dragonhall, bond 33.

Phillip read it three times. The copperplate was beautiful; somebody had ruled the columns with a straight edge and taken pride in the ruling.

It was not a debt cleared. He had been told his whole life that it was a debt cleared, and there was no debt anywhere on the page. Four years of a boy in. One boy's schooling out. The two figures balanced like a well-kept sum, and his life turned quietly round on its axis and settled facing a new direction.

At the end of the line, where the ink had bitten deepest, was his father's signature: short, plain, the T laid down in one heavy stroke, like a man setting something out of his hand.

"You have found the ninth page," said Bursar Ilsabet Quorn.

She was in the doorway, and had been for some time. She carried no lamp, which meant she had watched him read in the dark.

"You signed this," said Phillip.

"I authorised it. And the other forty." She set her hand flat on the shelf, and her voice never rose above the rain. "I shall not waste your time denying any part of it, Mr Vane. You can read. Ask."

"You bought my childhood to pay for my school."

"We bought four years of a lockkeeper's son and paid the whole of his schooling out of them. Your family kept you. They lost the earliest and least of you, and I have never once described it otherwise in this building."

"He didn't know what he was selling."

"He knew precisely what he was selling. He did not know what it would feel like afterwards, which is a different complaint, and a fair one." She glanced at the page the way you check an addition. "I sit with them while they sign. I have never delegated it."

"And it goes down the Sluice." Phillip's voice had gone short and polite, which is what happened to it under weight. "Into her. Into Ninefold. In order."

"Everything spent goes down. That is not policy, it is drainage." She faced him properly for the first time. "Now let me price it for you, since you came all this way for the arithmetic. Two ridges over, in my twenty-third year, a small accrual opened by itself. It handed four thousand people everything back in one night, all at once, out of order — every death, every shame, every buried thing together, with no time to take them one at a time. I was there. I carried water. Ninety-one did not survive the week, and most of those died of nothing a doctor could write down."

Downstairs, the clock ticked.

"Ours is nine galleries and two hundred years. That would cost the valley four hundred lives, Mr Vane. I have done the work. Have you?"

He had not. That was the trouble. He stood with his father's signature under his hand and could not find the sum that beat hers, because she had not lied to him once and was not enjoying any of it.

"Then you will want the rest," said Ilsabet, in exactly the same tone, which made it worse. "You are the most willing spender to come through this Hall in thirty years. I noted it at your Assize and every week since. We shall move you to Reckoner at the winter accounts, early, no examination. Auditor before you are twenty, and an Auditor's spending is unlimited by charter."

"And then?"

"Then, one day, when the pressure requires it, the binding. It takes one person, spending down to nothing, to hold nine galleries shut. It is the most useful thing any human being in Ilmarch will ever do, and the school does not order it. It is offered." She let that stand. "We call it the honour."

It did not sound like a sentence. It sounded like being loved, and Phillip understood that this was the point of it, and that knowing so did not make him safe from it.

"I'd want to think," he said.

"You would be the first who did," said Ilsabet Quorn, and stepped out of the doorway.

He did not take his own entry. He turned the volume back to the front instead, and tore out the schedule leaf on his way past, slowly, along the gutter, so that it came away whole. Forty-one lines. A number, a name, a place in the sequence, a date. Everything the book had, in the order the book had taken it, on one sheet the size of his two hands. He folded it twice and put it inside his coat. It simply seemed wrong that it should stay in a book.

Out on the step, in the gate lamp's small yellow room of light, he stopped. There would be a report; there always was, and the porter's out-book would carry two names, and one of them was Bellhook.

Ilsabet had followed him out and stood under the lintel with her hands folded.

Phillip knelt and drew the tally-sigil on the wet stone. Then he said his coin out loud, more of it than the work required, because saying it was the last time he would have it.

"The winter the basin froze," he said. "I was nine. He came up the towpath at four in the morning with the storm lamp and got me out of bed with my boots on the wrong feet, and walked me out onto the black basin, which nobody does, with his fist in the back of my collar. It was so cold the inside of my nose hurt. The ice went off under us in long notes, like somebody tuning something enormous — and every time it went, he laughed. Put his head back and laughed out loud at it. That is the only time in my life I have heard my father laugh, and I am spending it to keep a name out of a book."

The chalk went cold. Down in the lodge, two leaves of the out-book bound quietly to one another and would turn as one through every spring audit, for as long as the paper lasted.

Phillip stood up. He knew, as a fact, that his father had laughed once.

He could not hear it.

Ilsabet Quorn watched from the lintel and wrote nothing down. He did not understand until much later that she had no need to: every coin he spent made him fitter for what she wanted him for.

The rain stopped before dawn and hard cold came down behind it. He walked up the Cold Gantry road with frost stiffening in the ruts, the black basin lying flat and faintly steaming below, four hundred houses with their curtains drawn.

At Gantry Lock Four there was a lamp lit in the window.

His father was up, then; the water does not care what hour it is. Phillip stood at the end of the lock beam with the torn page cold against his ribs and looked at the yellow square of the window, and at the door beside it, which he knew was painted and whose colour he could not see.

He did not go in. He had built four first sentences on the way up that road, and every one ended in a question he had no business making that man answer at six in the morning.

Nessa was at the top of the hill with a lantern she had been holding a long time; her arm had gone down to her side and the light made a small useless pool at her feet. She asked him nothing. She looked at his face and started walking, and he fell in beside her, and neither of them said a word.

Then, up the valley, from the Hall, the Counting Bell began to strike.

It was not the hour. There was nobody in the tower; the ringers were abed and the rope was tied off. The bell rang anyway, one stroke for every hundred coins spent.

It struck six. Then seven. Then it went past nine, and past twelve, and kept on, steady, unhurried, in the frozen dark, and did not stop.

## Session 6 — In Arrears

The bell did not stop at the hour. It went on into a number nobody had ever heard, and Phillip and Nessa ran up the valley road with the frost breaking under their boots like sugar, and behind them Lock Four's window went small and yellow and then went out of sight altogether.

"That's past ninety," Nessa said. Her breath tore. "Nobody spends ninety hundred in a morning. Vane. Nobody."

"I know."

They came up through the lower gate into a Hall that was already wrong. It was in the air first. The Sill breath had always lived down in the boards, a faint warm dryness like a bread oven three hours cold; now it stood at chest height in the courtyard, thick as fog and just as visible, and it smelled less like bread and more like the inside of an empty tin. The frost was going off the roofs where it had no business going: meltwater ran off the slates in ropes and steamed where it hit the flags, because the mountain was heating the building from underneath. The cloister lamps had gone furry and orange. And under everything, through the soles of his feet, Phillip could feel the mountain doing something patient and enormous, the way a barge comes through a lock wall long before you see it.

The Blank was coming up the Sluice.

You could tell where it had been because nothing was marked. That was the horror of it. The east stair was perfectly ordinary, and eleven fourth-floor students sat on it in their grey wool, in no distress at all, each of them looking at their own hands, turning them over, patient, waiting for somebody to explain whose those were.

"Don't touch them and don't ask them anything," said Judd the porter, going past with an armful of blankets. "Tally Floor. Everyone down to the Tally Floor."

The Hall emptied downward. Three hundred children under a carved motto reading DEBTS ARE PAID BY THOSE WHO OWE THEM, and no nibs going, no sleet-sound, and that silence was worse than the bell.

Hew Marrable came up out of the north stairhead with grit in his eyebrows and his coat steaming. He did not waste anything, not breath, not time.

"Vane. Her ladyship's seams have started. She's off her feed and she's swelling and the brick's letting go along the throat. I need hands that have been below and didn't run. That's a very short list, lad, and you're the only Quire on it."

"How long?"

"Days, if we pack her. Hours, if we don't." Hew looked at him a moment longer than was comfortable. "Aye, well. You can say no. Say it now, in the light, where it's cheap."

Phillip did not say no. He had never once said no in his life, and he was not going to learn it in the light.

The Sumps were hot. That was new. Thirty years of Hew's careful cold, and now the galleries had a fever in them, the walls sweating warm grit that came away glittering on your palm. At Gallery Nine the sound was the worst of it: a slow, thick creak, like a great book being turned by somebody with all the time in the world.

Ninefold filled the dark. Hide of compacted slate and pressed paper, warm as a loaf, scored everywhere with hairline seams — and along the flank, three of those seams had opened a finger's width, and light was coming out of them. Not flame. Nothing under Barrow Sill has ever burned. It was the pale, moving light of silt under pressure, and where it touched the roof the brick began, very gently, to forget how to be a roof.

"Bracing reckoning," Hew said. "Along the seam, hold to hold, like stitching a sack. You draw. I'll pack."

Phillip drew the tally-sigil in wet grit and put both hands on the hide and paid.

"The first thaw at Lock Four," he said, and his voice went steady the way it always did, because saying it was the last time he would ever have it. "I was nine. The ice went out of the pound overnight and it came down against the gates all at once, a whole winter of it, and it sounded like every plate in Ilmarch going off a table at the same moment. There was a slab of it the size of a door standing on its edge, green through the middle, and the sun came up behind it and it went the colour of a bottle. And my father was up on the beam in his shirt with no coat on, in February, whistling at it — two long and one short, the way you whistle a barge through — and it fell over, and the water came up over my boots and it was so cold it was hot."

The last word went out of him. The seam drew shut a hand's width with a sound like a wet rope tightening.

He knew, afterwards, that he had been nine and there had been ice. He could not have told you one colour of it.

"Good," said Hew. "Again. Two feet along."

He paid again. He put his palms back on the warm hide, and he took the best thing he had left, because he always did, and because saying it properly was the only funeral it was going to get.

"The towpath under Bellhook's lock, August," he said. "We'd walked out past the lime kilns and the whole cut was low and green and stinking sweetly, and the flies stood up off it in columns you could put your hand through. Nessa had gone in after a boathook — went in on purpose, off the coping, no fuss — and she had her coat spread out on the hot stone drying, and wet wool in the sun has a smell like a sheep apologising. There was a barge coming up called the Pity of Ware with the E painted backwards, and I said something about it. I don't know what I said. I never did know, because she laughed before I got to the end of it. She always laughed early. Half a second before the joke landed, at the shape of it coming, so you were never sure she'd properly heard you — hoarse at the bottom, going up at the end like a question, and she put one hand flat on the stone to hold herself up, and a moorhen went off across the water like a thrown boot. She laughed at me the whole length of the pound and never once told me why."

The last word left him and the seam drew shut another foot.

What was left was a sentence, the way a sentence is left. Nessa Bellhook laughs before the joke lands. There was nothing at all underneath it.

He reached for the next coin and found the shelf swept.

"There's — " He stopped. "Hold on. I've got something."

"Take your time, lad."

He did not have time and he did not have something. That was arrears, and he knew the shape of it now, the way you know a stair that is not there.

Then Odd was in the gallery.

Nobody had sent for him. He came down the ramp in his Reckoner's grey, cuffs buttoned, unhurried, and looked at the open seam the way a surgeon looks at a table.

"Marrable, third edition, chapter nine," Odd said. "A bracing at the — at the narrow part, sir. The throat. It wants three hands, not two. Section four. It is in the — " a small silence, arriving nowhere near where it belonged " — in the book, sir, whatever you say about the book."

"Pell — "

"I am the best in this building at the thing you are doing badly." He said it without heat, the way you would read out a weight. "Vane. Draw. I shall spend."

And he spent. He set his fingertips on the hide the way a man sets a cup down on a saucer, and he began, in that reedy, metronome voice, with the little silences falling in all the wrong places.

"The summer I was twelve. At Aldermew," Odd said. "They let the — the long water with the stone edge down every August, so the weed dies back, and for a month the whole lawn smells of warm mud and mint and nobody minds it, and the gardeners walk about in the empty bottom of it looking for the things people have dropped in since the last time. I had the room at the top, under the — under the glass. You could hear rain start up there four or five seconds before it reached the ground, so I always knew first. There was a dog that was not permitted upstairs and came upstairs. Brown, and one ear had been bitten through when he was young and healed folded over, and he would put his whole head across my feet while I worked and go to sleep, and my feet would go numb, and I would not move them, because — " a small silence, arriving nowhere near where it belonged " — because I did not want to. And at ten at night the woman who does the soup sent a boy up with bread and butter and hot milk with the skin lifted off it, and one night she brought it herself, and she stood in the doorway holding the — the flat thing with the handles, and she said, you are a good boy and I am sorry how they work you. I have not required anyone to say it since."

The last word went out of him, and the light in the seam narrowed by a finger's width, and the gallery was very quiet.

"Odd," said Phillip. "What was the dog called?"

Odd considered the flank with mild professional interest.

"There was a dog," he said. "At the place with my sponsor's name on it. One summer." The pause fell in the wrong place again. "It is not material. Two feet along, if you please."

And he said another, and another, each one entire, precise, unhurried, spoken to a wall of hide in a voice like a clerk reading out weights — a woman's face, feature by feature, the brow and the two lines beside the mouth and the way she had of looking at the ceiling before she answered you. He did not pause between them. He did not once decline. And Phillip, greying at the edges, needing the help so badly he could taste it, did not stop him. Not once. He had believed since Michaelmas that this was what love looked like when it was any use.

"Odd. That's enough now. That's enough."

"There is no such quantity."

"Odd — "

"Vane. Draw."

And somewhere in the fifth or sixth of them a voice came up through every Sluice grate in the Hall at once — through the Tally Floor gratings, through the dormitory boards, through the drain in the kitchen yard where the cook stood with a colander in her hands — and three hundred children heard it, and every one of them went quiet, because it was a small boy's voice, and it said a name in a way that was clearly loving.

Every person in Dragonhall knew, in their bones, that they had heard something they ought to recognise.

Phillip Vane was the only one in the building who did not recognise it at all.

The seam took. The brick held. And Odalric Pell stood back from the flank with his cuffs still buttoned and looked pleasantly around the gallery, and said, "That should hold. Tell the — the one with my name on it. Tell him I did it properly."

"Odd," said Phillip. "Say your name."

"I have just," said Odd, "said it."

The Auditors came within the quarter hour, four of them, calm as weather, with sealing chalk and a stretcher nobody was in a hurry to use. They took Odd by the elbows and he went with perfect courtesy. Nessa was standing at the gallery mouth with a lantern and a new assistant's armband on her sleeve, and she had been there long enough to see all of it: who came uninvited, who was drawing, who never said stop.

The senior Auditor asked, in a voice like a form being filled in, whether anyone present had witnessed the spending, and who had authorised it.

Nessa looked at the floor.

She kept her post. That was the whole of it. Nobody argued; the room simply moved on, the way rooms do. She lifted her lantern to light them out, because that was her duty now, and she did it well.

"Nessa."

"Vane." Her voice had nothing in it. Her consonants were clipped down to nails. "Don't."

"You saw."

"I know what I did," she said. "Don't you dare tell me I don't." And then, because she could not manage his first name and had never once tried, "Vane," again, and she turned and held the light steady for the men taking Odd up the ramp.

He stood and watched her do it and tried, without meaning to, to remember what she sounded like laughing. He got the sentence and nothing under it — and he had done that himself, an hour ago, with his own mouth, to hold up a wall.

Hew Marrable had chosen before any of it happened. Given the seam or the boy, he packed the seam. He got Odd out afterward, and afterward was too late for a part of Odd that no ledger in Ilmarch would ever get back, and when Phillip said so, out loud, shaking, on the ramp, the old man did not pretend.

"That's right," Hew said. "Three times now. Twice before you were born." He sat down on the step with his hands hanging. "I've never once pretended, lad. It's the one decency I've got left, and it's not much of one."

Then, low, and shaking:

"She was fed on purpose. Whole lives, entire, in order — somebody sat down and worked out that a heap can't be unpacked but a story can, and they made her able to say I, and then they got frightened and bricked the gallery. She isn't a hazard. She's an archive that's learned to want. She could be read, one life at a time, out loud, till she's empty and everybody's got their own back. I've said it in print for thirty years." He wiped his face with the flat of his hand. "And nobody has ever let me try."

Arrears is a disciplinary matter at Dragonhall, not a medical one. Phillip was suspended pending review before the second bell, and walked down the valley road in the rain with his kit in a sack.

Tobin Vane was waiting at Lock Four with a coat over his arm two sizes too big, because he had bought it at Michaelmas for a boy he expected to grow.

"Right," he said. He did not ask. He put the coat round his son's shoulders and looked away down the cut. "Get that rope in, lad. She's been slack since Tuesday and I can't get down for her."

So Phillip got the rope in, hand over hand, the wet hemp warming under his palms; and when he straightened, he looked at the lock-house door.

He knew it was green. He knew it the way you know a date. There was nothing behind the word at all — no shade of it, no paint gone chalky at the bottom, no man standing in it — and he stood there in his father's enormous coat and could not get it back, because he had sold it in October in front of three hundred people and they had all called it magnificent.

The kitchen was warm. Rain went over the slates in long soft handfuls, the stove ticked, and Tobin fell asleep in the chair with his bad knee up on the fender and his mouth a little open. Phillip sat awake in the borrowed coat with a torn ledger page against his ribs and a stranger's thirty seconds in his pocket.

He had it all now and none of it helped. Ninefold would open within days. The school had a plan, and the plan was a person: one name, spending down and down until nothing was left standing in the shape of them. Ilsabet Quorn had written his name against it in a small book last October, and had been buying him ever since, a coin at a time, and calling it an honour.

Sometime after midnight the dogs started.

Not one dog. Every dog in Cold Gantry, four hundred houses of them, coming awake in the same half-second, Basin Row to the chapels to the far end of the wharf — and then no barking at all, just four hundred animals standing in the dark, facing the same way.

Phillip went to the window. The rain had stopped. And the whole valley, down to the black basin where the canal ended, smelled faintly and unmistakably of a bread oven gone out.

Out on the water, in the middle of the night, in November, a thin white mist was lifting off the basin in slow coils.

The water was going warm.

## Session 7 — The Reading

The rain had stopped in the small hours and left the slates ticking as they dried. Tobin Vane slept in the chair with his bad knee up on the fender and his mouth a little open, and Phillip stood in the kitchen doorway of the lock-house in a coat two sizes too big for him and looked at his father for as long as he could afford to.

The room smelled of tallow and wet wool and cold ash, and under all of it, faint as a hand on the back of the neck, the other smell: a bread oven gone out. It had come down the valley at dusk and settled over four hundred houses, and no living person in Cold Gantry had smelled it this far down before. Nobody said the word for it.

He did not leave a note. Notes get read.

He went up the valley in the last black hour before dawn, past the basin where the canal was steaming like a bath, past houses with lamps still burning at four in the morning because nobody in the town had gone to bed after the water went warm. Dogs stood in yards with their ears up and did not bark. The frost on the towpath had come in wrong, half-melted, greasy underfoot. Barrow Sill sat in cloud above him, and the whole mountain breathed out slow and dry and warm, the way something breathes when it has been holding on a long while and is nearly done.

The ledger page was folded flat against his chest inside the coat. Ada Tull's scruple was in his right pocket, where it had been since Michaelmas, small and lensed and cool as a button.

They were waiting at the Hall gate, which he had expected, and they had made it beautiful, which he had not.

The courtyard flags had been swept. The tally-sigil was already drawn on them, twelve feet across, laid by somebody with a rule and a steady hand. A lectern stood at the head of it with the paperwork under a brass weight, already signed. Three hundred students lined the cloister in grey wool with their fingerless gloves off, which they only did for oaths and funerals, and every one of them was looking at him the way they had on the Tally Floor at Michaelmas, when he held a hundredweight of iron off the boards and paid four times over for it.

Bursar Ilsabet Quorn came down the steps with her hands folded and her collar buttoned to the throat.

"Good morning, Mr Vane. Thank you for being punctual. It saves the valley an hour, and an hour is worth a great deal this week."

"Ma'am."

"I will not dress it. Accrual Nine opens tonight or tomorrow. A binding requires one voice and one account, spoken until there is nothing left in the account to speak. What is new is the schedule of consideration." She lifted the top sheet with two fingers. "Miss Bellhook keeps her assistantship and her family stays off the water. Mr Pell keeps his sponsorship and his care, which he will need for the rest of his life and cannot otherwise afford. Your father is never told what this cost or who paid it. That last is not a bribe, Mr Vane. It is a mercy, and I have priced it honestly."

The chalk lay on the lectern's shelf. Fresh stick, unbroken.

"You've done the work," Phillip said.

"I have done the work. Four hundred lives in the town, against one account. I did that sum forty years ago in a valley two ridges from here, and I was twenty-three, and I got it wrong by being late."

He looked at the sigil then, properly. Twelve feet of it, ruled and swept and beautiful, and at the centre of it a space about the size of a boy. Three hundred faces above it under the cloister arches, waiting to watch him be magnificent one last time. Nessa Bellhook was four arches down with the assistant's armband on her sleeve, and she did not look away from him, and she did not come out of the line either, and both of those cost her something.

"No," Phillip said.

He had not planned to say it and it came out at ordinary volume, and the courtyard was so quiet that it went all the way up to the cloister roof and came back down again. Somewhere along the line a boy said his name out loud, appalled, the way you say a name when somebody drops a tray in chapel. A wind came over the wall and lifted chalk dust off the sigil in a thin grey curl.

Ilsabet Quorn did not raise her voice. She never had.

"That is a refusal, Mr Vane, and a refusal is only an answer when you have something to set in its place. Have you?"

He did not.

"Then you have not declined the price. You have moved it. Four hundred people down that road will pay it instead of you, tonight or tomorrow, and not one of them has been asked, and not one of them will ever know your name." She let that sit exactly as long as it needed to. "I will have it entered in the register, with the hour. Every boy on this cloister will remember where he was standing when you said it. I am sorry about that. Publicity is not something I can refund you."

"Can I see her first."

Ilsabet looked at him with her cool grey courtesy and found nothing to charge for the request. "Auditor Marrable will take you down." She took the fresh stick of chalk off the lectern's shelf, put it into his hand, and closed his fingers over it herself, and her hand was cold and perfectly steady. "Take that with you. Please be quick."

The Sump stair was warmer than it had ever been. Hew Marrable went ahead with the lamp swinging and said nothing for six flights, and at the gallery mouth he stopped and put a hand flat on the doorframe like a man steadying a horse.

Gallery Nine was full of light and it was the wrong kind. Ninefold lay along the floor of it, sixty feet of hide like slate and pressed paper, every hairline seam in her shining — no heat in it beyond the loaf-warmth she always had, only a dry silver glitter packed into the cracks like frost in a wall, silt under so much pressure it had begun to give off light. The air tasted of iron-gall ink and old flour. Eleven eyes came round, slowly, and settled on him, all of them, the way they had the first time.

"She's off her feed," Hew said, low. "She's past feed. Mind her seams, lad, don't touch her at the seams."

Ninefold spoke, and for one sentence it was the usual chorus, a bargeman's vowels sliding into an old woman's, a clerk's flat consonants, a laugh belonging to nobody in the room.

"— and it does not come up. Down, and down. We are what the school throws away —"

Then it stopped, the whole braided crowd of them, cut off mid-clause.

And in the enormous quiet a small voice said, clearly:

"I."

Phillip's hand went to the wall behind him.

It was a child's voice, three years old at the most, unhurried, pleased with itself — the voice of somebody standing in a warm kitchen with the door open behind them. He knew it the way you know your own handwriting in a stranger's papers. He had never heard it in his life. He had never been allowed to.

"They fed her whole ones," Hew said. His own voice had gone to pieces. "Heaps can't be unpacked. A life in order can. Forty-one, in sequence, on purpose."

Phillip's hand was already inside his coat. He got the torn page out and opened it flat in both hands, and Hew brought the lamp over without being asked, and he ran his thumb down the copperplate to the line he had known by heart since the Spendhouse and had never once said aloud.

"Thirty-three," he said. "Vane, Tobin. Lockkeeper, Gantry Lock Four. Sold: the subject's first four years, entire and in sequence." He looked up into the eleven eyes. "That's mine. That's me she's talking with."

Hew Marrable stood there with his mouth open and the lamp going down slowly to the end of his arm.

"Thirty years," he said at last. "Thirty years I've fed her and mucked her seams and written the book on her, and nobody ever once told me what was in the bucket."

Eleven eyes did not blink.

He was not the dragon's victim and never had been. His first four years had gone down the Sluice in a numbered lot, in order, and out of them a thing under a mountain had assembled a self. He was the ingredient.

He thought, distantly, that he ought to feel robbed. What he felt instead was a small boy in a doorway, still standing there, still waiting to be spoken to.

He put the chalk down on the flagstones. He had carried it down six flights of stair without once noticing it was in his hand, which told him something about himself he did not much enjoy knowing.

Then he knelt on the warm stone of Gallery Nine with the page open across his knee, and did not draw anything at all.

The page was for the forty-one. The first name he had in his hand. He took Ada Tull's scruple out of his right pocket and closed his bare fingers round it, and it warmed to him at once, the way it had in the Sluice three seasons ago — flour, and a burnt pan, and a doorway full of light — thirty seconds of a woman he had never met, carried about in a boy's coat for half a year like a pebble off a beach.

"Ada Tull," he read. "Of Basin Row, Cold Gantry."

His voice was not steady and he did not wait for it to be.

"There's flour on her hands to the wrist. The pan's gone black at the bottom and she's cross about it, but not properly cross, because it's only a pan. There's a red cloth folded on the sill. There's a doorway with the light of the yard beyond it, and she's calling a child in, and she says the name twice — and the second time it's got the small rising bend at the end that means now."

Nothing struck. Nothing thundered. A seam near the great flank went dark, the way a coal goes out, and along the whole body the light dropped by a fraction so small you had to be watching for it.

The pressure had gone down by exactly one life.

The stone in his hand cooled and went ordinary, clear and dull as a bit of bottle glass off the towpath, because there was nothing in it any more. It had gone home. Down in the town, on Basin Row, a woman of sixty stopped in her own doorway with her hands in the flour and stood quite still while her kitchen came back to her — the black pan, the light through the door, her daughter's name called across the yard and laughed at halfway through. Ada Tull sat down on her own step with her floury hands over her face. She had never known there was anything gone. She had only known that she could not make bread the way she used to, and had blamed the flour for eleven years.

Behind him Hew Marrable sat down on the gallery floor with the lamp still in his hand and wept without any noise at all, because in thirty years nobody with a voice had ever once been let read her.

Then he stopped, all at once, the way a man stops when something arrives, and got up onto his knees.

"It hasn't closed," he said. "Lad. Look at your seam. It's gone dark and it's standing open."

He held his hand flat in the air an inch off the hide, not touching, the way he would have done with a horse.

"Thirty years I've packed her seams shut, and every seam I packed put the pressure somewhere else, and I called that husbandry. That's all a rupture is. That's all it ever was — pressure with nowhere to be. She isn't opening because she's full, she's opening because she's *shut*." His voice went out from under him and he dragged it back. "You've cut a tap in her. One name wide. She can bleed in order now, and a thing that bleeds in order can't burst."

"How long does that hold her?"

"As long as you keep on," Hew said. "And not one hour past that."

A yard along the great flank a seam that had been dull came up bright, the way a lamp is turned up in the next room, with something in it waiting to be said.

"They're not beasts," Phillip said, to the room, to the school over his head. "They're people, filed."

Then he read the second name.

They came for him a little before eleven that morning, and the school's one move was the one it had always had, which was paperwork with legs. Four Auditors down the Sump stair in good coats, unhurried, wiping the wet off their spectacles, with canvas rolls of sealing chalk under their arms — the fat grey sticks that are used for closing a gallery, which is not a lock and works far better than one, because a sealed doorway stops being a doorway and forgets how to be walked through. It is an expensive reckoning. An Auditor's spending is unlimited by charter. That is why Auditors are what you send.

Hew went and stood in the gallery mouth, and was taken out of it, politely, by two men who had been his students, one of them holding his elbow the whole way and saying sorry, sir, in a low voice, over and over, like a boy at a funeral.

Then Nessa Bellhook came down the stair behind them.

She did not make a speech. She put her back against the doorframe and her arms out along it and said the only thing in the world that could have worked, which was not brave and was not even loud. It was a procedure.

"Gallery Nine is under audit. You can't seal a gallery that's under audit — Bursar's own standing order, nineteen, subsection four. I'm the assistant on the audit. I opened it at eight this morning and I haven't signed it off."

She had opened it at eight that morning. She had gone into the office in the dark and written it into the register herself and dated it, which took her about ninety seconds and cost her a career.

One of the Auditors went back up for a ruling. Rulings take time, in a school where the clerks have begun not to come in. By the time anything came back down there were sixty people from Cold Gantry on the Sump stair in their wet coats, sitting on the steps in the dry warm dark, waiting for their names, and you cannot seal a gallery with sixty ratepayers in the way of it.

Nessa did not look at Phillip while she did any of it, and she did not take the armband off, not that day. She was still going to need it.

It took the rest of that day and did not stop after. It could not be done quietly, which was the whole cost of it: a life goes back to whoever lost it, wherever they are standing, in front of whoever is standing with them. By noon a wool factor in Ilmarch's finest street had sat down on his own stairs with everything he had paid the Spendhouse to take. The Quietage's clients got their griefs back and their crimes came with them, in company, and there is no discreet way to hand a man an afternoon he bought his way out of. Within a fortnight the Ledger Houses' paper on Dragonhall was worth nothing at all. The school's money ended, and the school with it, slowly, the way a barge takes on water.

Ilsabet Quorn came down on the second morning, stood at the gallery mouth where her four Auditors had failed to get past a girl of fifteen and a line in a register, and did not stop him. She had sent chalk once. She did not send it twice. Phillip glanced up and saw her doing the sum, four hundred against one against the whole ruined arithmetic of eighty years — and saw her arrive at the bottom of it and stand there.

"I cannot feel the answer," she said. It was the only time in her life she repeated herself. "I know it. I cannot feel it. I have not been able to for a very long while, and I did not notice until now."

Nessa came down on the fourth day and did not go back up. She took the assistant's armband off on the stair and left it folded on the step, where it stayed for a week and then was gone, and nobody ever asked who took it. She did not explain herself. She took the lamp out of Hew's hands and held it steady over the page for eleven hours, which cost her the post and her family's dry roof and every plan she had ever made, and which was the only apology available to her.

"Read on, Phillip," she said.

On the sixth night she nearly got away from him.

He was two clauses into a bargeman named Sowerby — a coil of new rope, a dog asleep on a cabin roof, a girl standing at a lock gate in the rain with her hood down — when the whole gallery came up bright as a forge yard. A seam over his head opened wider than a read seam has any business opening and went on opening, a hand's width, two, silt glaring out of it like sun off water, and the air went so dry so fast that his tongue stuck to the roof of his mouth. That was the Blank coming up out of her the way a wave comes up a slipway, and it was ten feet above his head, and on the steps behind him sixty people from Cold Gantry stood up all together without a sound.

"Don't stop," Hew said, from the floor, absolutely level. "Whatever else you do, don't stop. And don't you dare go faster."

Because stopping shuts the tap. And hurrying is worse than stopping, because a blurred name buys nothing anywhere in Ilmarch, and a name half said is a seam half opened.

So he went slower. He said the dog's ears, one up and one folded over. He said the smell of wet rope, which is not like dry rope. He said the exact tin rattle the lock gate made under the girl's hand, and how she was not pretty and knew it and did not care that morning. He was so frightened that his voice came out flat and careful, like a boy reading a lesson, and it turned out that flat and careful is precisely what the work wants.

The seam did not close. Nothing he read ever closed. But it stopped widening, and hung there, and then it went dark from one end like a wick drowning, and the gallery went back down to its ordinary bread-oven dark, and Nessa's hands on the lamp were shaking so hard that Hew reached up and took hold of her wrist and steadied it for her without a word.

After that he read six hours on and two off, all night and every night, and never once let the reading fall behind her. Twice more that winter she got ahead of him, and twice more the answer was the same one: slower, and truer.

On the ninth day, up out of the dark, one noun came back where it belonged. Odd Pell was sitting on the steps with his hands in his lap when a voice that was not his own said Odalric into the gallery, and he answered to it. Not all of him came back. Enough did to know his own name when it was handed up to him out of the dark, and to say it afterwards without stopping in the middle.

The reading went on into the winter. People came up from Cold Gantry and sat on the Sump steps in their coats and waited for their names, and the town organised it themselves in the end, because the Hall had no clerks left to do it.

With one line left unread on the page, on a night when the lamp was low, Ninefold gave him something he had not asked for.

It came as a smell first, then whole and entire: a morning after a signing, eleven years old, his father sitting out on the lock beam in the wet with a folded paper in both hands, crying, not making any sound about it. The gift he had offered freely and kept and then given away. It came back the way nothing bought ever does.

Phillip held it, and found that he could. It hurt exactly as much as it always had, and he could carry it, and knowing that was the last thing he needed.

He had come to his own number weeks before that. Thirty-three, in its place, in sequence, with the date beside it in somebody's tidy hand and eight more entries still under it. He had gone straight past it that night and read thirty-four instead, and finished the eight, and come back up the page, and gone past it again, and again, and again, until there was nobody left above it and nobody left below it and the paper had gone soft as cloth at the folds and thirty-three sat alone in the middle of it like a stone in a cleared field.

Then he read the name he had been turning past for a month.

"Tobin Vane. Lockkeeper. Gantry Lock Four."

Four years went back, entire, into a man standing at the gallery mouth in his work coat with his cap in his hands, because he had walked up the valley without being sent for and nobody had the heart to stop him. They went back in order, which is the only survivable way: a boy at one, at two, at three, the kitchen, the boots left out to dry, the yard, the small voice in the doorway. And on the end of it, in its proper place, the afternoon he sat at a table in the Spendhouse and signed his son away for a future, because the alternative was the water.

And the doorway came back green. He had sold that green at Michaelmas to be magnificent, and it came home to him now — wet paint, a warped bottom rail, his father filling the frame of it — at the exact moment his father was finding him.

Tobin Vane looked at his son and found something there. All of it.

"Phillip," he said.

They would both have to live with it. That was the point.

Months on, the Sump floor at Gallery Nine looks like this. One lamp, turned low, because the seams give a little light of their own now and there is no money for oil. A page, gone soft as cloth at the folds. A boy sitting cross-legged on warm stone with a coat over his knees, reading aloud, slowly, the way you read to somebody falling asleep.

On the steps behind him, people from Cold Gantry, waiting for their names. Somebody has brought a flask. Somebody's child has fallen asleep against the rail. Three steps up sits Ada Tull with a basket and a cloth over it, because she brings bread down on the nights she is able, and on a string round her neck she wears a little dull lens of glass with nothing whatever left inside it, which he put into her hand himself and which she would not be parted from. The Quietage is finished and Dragonhall is finished and the Counting Bell has not been rung since autumn, and Phillip Vane owns nothing, holds a place nowhere, and has not spent a single coin.

He turns the page.

There are eight more galleries under Barrow Sill, all of them full, none of them read. The next name on the list is not from Cold Gantry at all.

He clears his throat, and begins.
