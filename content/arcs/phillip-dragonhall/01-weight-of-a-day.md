The pig-iron came onto the Tally Floor at first light on a barrow with a bad wheel, and all three hundred of them heard it coming before they saw it. A hundredweight, cast in one dull block the colour of a wet road, and when the porters tipped it onto the boards the floor took the weight the way a boat takes a wave. Dust jumped. Somebody's inkwell walked half an inch to the left and stopped.

Phillip Vane looked at it and began working out what it was going to cost him.

That was the whole of the Michaelmas Assize. Lift the iron off the boards, hold it for a count of sixty, and pay for it out of your own head. Every student in the hall had spent the last week deciding what they could bear to part with, and not one of them had said so out loud.

It was the coldest morning of the year so far. The Tally Floor was grey stone with a roof too high to heat, and above the great doors the school's motto was cut a foot deep into the lintel: DEBTS ARE PAID BY THOSE WHO OWE THEM. Rain moved across the high windows in long sheets. It smelled of chalk dust and beeswax and iron-gall ink, sharp as a coin held too long in a hand, and under all of it, faint and dry and warm, the smell the Hall never quite lost: a bread oven that had gone out some time in the night. The Sill breath. Nobody at Dragonhall ever mentioned it.

Three hundred students sat in grey wool and fingerless gloves, writing out their working, and the sound of it was sleet. Three hundred nibs, none of them in step. Phillip Vane had loved that sound since his first week and had never told a living soul, because there is no version of saying it out loud that does not make you sound like an idiot.

Nessa Bellhook sat on his left with her boots hooked round the bench legs and her hands flat on the desk, which was what she did instead of fidgeting.

"Hundredweight. Off the boards. Count of sixty. That's a slack rope, Vane. Sixty's long," Nessa said, and did not once look at the iron while she said it.

"Sixty's honest. They priced it fair. They usually do," Phillip said. He heard how that sounded, one beat too late, and added, "Which is the worst thing about them."

Nessa did not laugh, which told him everything about how her morning was going.

They called him first. Alphabetical was Bellhook before Vane, but the invigilating Auditor had a list of her own. Auditor Crake went down it quickly, ticking, and did not look up when she said his name.

"Vane," she said. "Bond thirty-three. Chalk your sigil and pay."

He knelt on the boards. The chalk was cold. He drew the tally-sigil the way you are taught in Tally year — the ring, the crossbar, the little hook at the end that closes the account — and set his palm flat inside it, and looked at the iron, and began.

"The winch handle at Gantry Lock Four, in November, before the sun's up," Phillip said.

The nibs stopped. Three hundred of them, all at once. That was the part nobody warned you about in first year: the Tally Floor goes silent when somebody spends, because everybody wants to hear what a thing is worth.

"It's iron and it's been out in the weather since my grandfather, so it's got a skin of rust on it like felt, and under the felt it's smooth where four generations of hands have worn it. It's colder than the air. That's the thing about it. You come out of a warm kitchen into a cold yard and you think you know what cold is, and then you take that handle bare-handed and it takes the heat out of you like it's owed it. There's a nick on the underside of the grip that catches the pad of your thumb every fourth turn. Not enough to cut. Enough that you count by it. And it squeals on the second turn and only the second turn, every time, for eleven years, and my father says he'll grease it and he never does, because it's how he knows the gate's moving."

He heard his own voice go quiet at the end, because it always did.

"Cold iron. Rust like felt. The nick under my thumb. Every fourth turn."

And on the last word it went.

He kept the fact. He would know for the rest of his life that there had been a winch handle at Lock Four and that it was cold. He simply would not have it any more. It was a room with the furniture carried out.

The iron rose off the boards. It came up smooth, four inches, and hung in the grey light with rain-shadow crawling over it, and Auditor Crake began to count aloud, and Phillip knelt with his palm in the chalk and let the weight settle onto his chest, because that is where it settles. At sixty she said stop, and he let go, and the floor took the wave again.

The applause was polite. He was good at this. It was the one thing he was reliably good at, and it had cost him a cold handle at dawn, and he was already doing the arithmetic on what he had left.

Then they called Nessa.

She knelt where he had knelt and drew her sigil clean — rope hands, better than his, a girl who had worked her family's lock since she was seven. She put her palm down and said, in that fast clipped way of hers that stops as soon as the point is made:

"A wet Tuesday. Autumn. There was a bell going somewhere down the valley." A pause. "I think it was the foundry. It might have been the chapel."

Phillip felt his stomach drop before the iron did.

Because that is the whole cruelty of Reckoning: vagueness is worthless. A blurred memory buys nothing. The clearer a thing is, the more you love it, the more it purchases — which means the price of every door you want opened is the best thing you own. Nessa Bellhook had decided, somewhere in the last two years, that she would not spend anything she loved. Phillip had watched her decide it. He had thought it was strength.

The iron came up perhaps two inches, tilted, and slid.

Four seconds. He would keep those four seconds for the rest of his life, and he would keep them free, because nobody ever charges you for the bad ones.

One: the block going over at the corner, slow, the way a thing goes when it has decided.

Two: a Tally girl called Bettany Rowe crossing the floor behind the invigilator's table with an armful of chalk boxes, eleven years old, looking at the chalk and not at the room, because she had been told to hurry.

Three: Nessa's face, which did not move at all.

Four: Phillip's palm already flat on the boards outside his own sigil, and his mouth already open, and no time in the world to pick something cheap.

So he did not pick something cheap.

"The door of the lock-house at Gantry Lock Four is green," he said. "It's green like the water is green when the barges have stirred the bottom up. It was painted the summer I was six and my father did it badly, and there's a run in the paint by the latch where it went too thick and dried in a drip like a tear, and I used to put my thumbnail in it. The bottom two inches are bare wood because the canal comes up over the sill in March. And my father is standing in it. He fills it. He has to duck coming out and he never remembers to, so there's a shine on the lintel where his head's been. He's got the lamp behind him so I can't see his face, only the shape of him, and he's saying get that rope in, lad, and he's holding the door open with his boot because his hands are full, and the green of it is the first colour I ever knew the name of."

He heard his voice fold in the middle. He kept going, because you have to finish.

"Green. The drip by the latch. My father in the doorway with the light behind him."

Gone.

The iron stopped falling. It did not slow; it simply stopped, eight inches above the boards and eleven inches from Bettany Rowe's shoulder, and hung there like something remembering its manners. Phillip held it for the full count of sixty, because Auditor Crake had started counting and it did not occur to him to argue. Bettany walked out from under it, understood what had happened only when she was clear of it, and sat down on the floor.

The Tally Floor came apart. Three hundred students on their feet, benches going over, a noise like weather. Auditor Crake wrote one word on her list, and Phillip, letting the block down onto the boards, was close enough to read it upside down. Distinguished.

He was glowing. That was the honest word for it. It went through him warm and complete, and it did not last past the moment he tried to picture the door.

He knew the door was there. He knew he had a father and that his father stood in it. He reached for the colour and got the word green, which is a fact, and behind the word there was a doorway he could not see, with a shape in it that had no face.

Nessa got up off her knees. She had passed. Her name was on the list with his.

"Nessa —"

"Don't." She was already gathering her chalk. Her voice had gone flat and consonant-heavy. "Don't gantry me, Vane."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were." She looked at him then, properly, and whatever was in her face was not gratitude and was not anger and had no name he could find. "You'll want thanking. And I can't." A beat. "I don't know why. But I can't."

She went. He stood on the Tally Floor with three hundred people saying his name and could not think of a single thing to do with his hands.

The gallery ran the length of the hall, twelve feet up, and staff watched the Assize from it the way owners watch horses. Bursar Ilsabet Quorn stood at the rail with her gloves folded over one wrist — grey silk, spectacles on a chain, and a stillness that made the students below look like weather. She waited until the noise thinned, and then said, not loudly, in a voice like slate under cold water:

"Mr Vane."

The hall found quiet for her without being asked.

"Bond thirty-three," said Ilsabet Quorn. "The task was priced at one particular of ordinary weight. I have some experience of pricing. What you laid down was worth four times that, and you laid it down in under four seconds, without instruction, for a girl who had not asked you." She took a small black book from her sleeve, and a pencil, and wrote in it. It took her perhaps five words. "I have noted it."

"Thank you, Bursar," Phillip said, because his sentences went short and polite under pressure.

"It is not praise, Mr Vane. It is a record." She closed the book. "Talent is common in this valley. Willingness is not, and it appears on no examination we set." She put the book away. "Go and eat. You will be colder than you think."

She was right about that, and he did not think about the book again for a very long time.

The Counting Bell rang long that day — one stroke for every hundred coins the school laid out — and by evening the students had stopped counting and started listening to it the way you listen to rain.

That night was the coldest night of the year. The fourth-floor dormitory had eleven beds and one window that did not shut, and the boys slept in their stockings and their coats. Phillip lay awake and listened to the ten o'clock bell go past ten, and past twelve, and past fifteen, and stop at a number nobody in the room said out loud.

Then the warmth came up through the boards. It did that on still nights — the Sill breath, dry and dusty and faintly sweet, an oven cooling somewhere a very long way down. And under it, so low it was more floor than sound, came a noise like a great slow book being turned. One enormous page, lifting and settling.

Phillip got out of bed. The boards were freezing under his bare feet, and he stood in the dark between the beds with everyone else asleep, and he was thinking about a doorway he could not see, and before he could stop himself he said, out loud, to nobody:

"It was green. I know it was green."

The book turned again.

It came after he spoke. Not with him, not before. After, the way an answer comes after a question, and nearer than the first time, and the boards under his bare feet on the coldest night of the year were warm.
