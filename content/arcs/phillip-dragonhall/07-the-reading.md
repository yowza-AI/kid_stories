The rain had stopped in the small hours and left the slates ticking as they dried. Tobin Vane slept in the chair with his bad knee up on the fender and his mouth a little open, and Phillip stood in the kitchen doorway of the lock-house in a coat two sizes too big for him and looked at his father for as long as he could afford to.

The room smelled of tallow and wet wool and cold ash, and under all of it, faint as a hand on the back of the neck, the other smell: a bread oven gone out. It had come down the valley at dusk and settled over four hundred houses, and no living person in Cold Gantry had smelled it this far down before. Nobody said the word for it.

He did not leave a note. Notes get read.

He went up the valley in the last black hour before dawn, past the basin where the canal was steaming like a bath, past houses with lamps still burning at four in the morning because nobody in the town had gone to bed after the water went warm. Dogs stood in yards with their ears up and did not bark. The frost on the towpath had come in wrong, half-melted, greasy underfoot. Barrow Sill sat in cloud above him, and the whole mountain breathed out slow and dry and warm, the way something breathes when it has been holding on a long while and is nearly done.

The ledger page was folded flat against his chest inside the coat. Ada Tull's scruple was in his right pocket, where it had been since Michaelmas, small and lensed and cool as a button.

They were waiting at the Hall gate, which he had expected, and they had made it beautiful, which he had not.

The courtyard flags had been swept. The tally-sigil was already drawn on them, twelve feet across, laid by somebody with a rule and a steady hand. A lectern stood at the head of it with the paperwork under a brass weight, already signed. Three hundred students lined the cloister in grey wool with their fingerless gloves off, which they only did for oaths and funerals, and every one of them was looking at him the way they had on the Tally Floor at Michaelmas, when he held a hundredweight of iron off the boards and paid four times over for it.

Bursar Ilsabet Quorn came down the steps with her hands folded and her collar buttoned to the throat.

"Good morning, Mr Vane. Thank you for being punctual. It saves the valley an hour, and an hour is worth a great deal this week."

"Ma'am."

"I will not dress it. Accrual Nine opens tonight or tomorrow. A binding requires one voice and one account, spoken until there is nothing left in the account to speak. What is new is the schedule of consideration." She lifted the top sheet with two fingers. "Miss Bellhook keeps her assistantship and her family stays off the water. Mr Pell keeps his sponsorship and his care, which he will need for the rest of his life and cannot otherwise afford. Your father is never told what this cost or who paid it. That last is not a bribe, Mr Vane. It is a mercy, and I have priced it honestly."

The chalk lay on the lectern's shelf. Fresh stick, unbroken.

"You've done the work," Phillip said.

"I have done the work. Four hundred lives in the town, against one account. I did that sum forty years ago in a valley two ridges from here, and I was twenty-three, and I got it wrong by being late."

He looked at the sigil then, properly. Twelve feet of it, ruled and swept and beautiful, and at the centre of it a space about the size of a boy. Three hundred faces above it under the cloister arches, waiting to watch him be magnificent one last time. Nessa Bellhook was four arches down with the assistant's armband on her sleeve, and she did not look away from him, and she did not come out of the line either, and both of those cost her something.

"No," Phillip said.

He had not planned to say it and it came out at ordinary volume, and the courtyard was so quiet that it went all the way up to the cloister roof and came back down again. Somewhere along the line a boy said his name out loud, appalled, the way you say a name when somebody drops a tray in chapel. A wind came over the wall and lifted chalk dust off the sigil in a thin grey curl.

Ilsabet Quorn did not raise her voice. She never had.

"That is a refusal, Mr Vane, and a refusal is only an answer when you have something to set in its place. Have you?"

He did not.

"Then you have not declined the price. You have moved it. Four hundred people down that road will pay it instead of you, tonight or tomorrow, and not one of them has been asked, and not one of them will ever know your name." She let that sit exactly as long as it needed to. "I will have it entered in the register, with the hour. Every boy on this cloister will remember where he was standing when you said it. I am sorry about that. Publicity is not something I can refund you."

"Can I see her first."

Ilsabet looked at him with her cool grey courtesy and found nothing to charge for the request. "Auditor Marrable will take you down." She took the fresh stick of chalk off the lectern's shelf, put it into his hand, and closed his fingers over it herself, and her hand was cold and perfectly steady. "Take that with you. Please be quick."

The Sump stair was warmer than it had ever been. Hew Marrable went ahead with the lamp swinging and said nothing for six flights, and at the gallery mouth he stopped and put a hand flat on the doorframe like a man steadying a horse.

Gallery Nine was full of light and it was the wrong kind. Ninefold lay along the floor of it, sixty feet of hide like slate and pressed paper, every hairline seam in her shining — no heat in it beyond the loaf-warmth she always had, only a dry silver glitter packed into the cracks like frost in a wall, silt under so much pressure it had begun to give off light. The air tasted of iron-gall ink and old flour. Eleven eyes came round, slowly, and settled on him, all of them, the way they had the first time.

"She's off her feed," Hew said, low. "She's past feed. Mind her seams, lad, don't touch her at the seams."

Ninefold spoke, and for one sentence it was the usual chorus, a bargeman's vowels sliding into an old woman's, a clerk's flat consonants, a laugh belonging to nobody in the room.

"— and it does not come up. Down, and down. We are what the school throws away —"

Then it stopped, the whole braided crowd of them, cut off mid-clause.

And in the enormous quiet a small voice said, clearly:

"I."

Phillip's hand went to the wall behind him.

It was a child's voice, three years old at the most, unhurried, pleased with itself — the voice of somebody standing in a warm kitchen with the door open behind them. He knew it the way you know your own handwriting in a stranger's papers. He had never heard it in his life. He had never been allowed to.

"They fed her whole ones," Hew said. His own voice had gone to pieces. "Heaps can't be unpacked. A life in order can. Forty-one, in sequence, on purpose."

Phillip's hand was already inside his coat. He got the torn page out and opened it flat in both hands, and Hew brought the lamp over without being asked, and he ran his thumb down the copperplate to the line he had known by heart since the Spendhouse and had never once said aloud.

"Thirty-three," he said. "Vane, Tobin. Lockkeeper, Gantry Lock Four. Sold: the subject's first four years, entire and in sequence." He looked up into the eleven eyes. "That's mine. That's me she's talking with."

Hew Marrable stood there with his mouth open and the lamp going down slowly to the end of his arm.

"Thirty years," he said at last. "Thirty years I've fed her and mucked her seams and written the book on her, and nobody ever once told me what was in the bucket."

Eleven eyes did not blink.

He was not the dragon's victim and never had been. His first four years had gone down the Sluice in a numbered lot, in order, and out of them a thing under a mountain had assembled a self. He was the ingredient.

He thought, distantly, that he ought to feel robbed. What he felt instead was a small boy in a doorway, still standing there, still waiting to be spoken to.

He put the chalk down on the flagstones. He had carried it down six flights of stair without once noticing it was in his hand, which told him something about himself he did not much enjoy knowing.

Then he knelt on the warm stone of Gallery Nine with the page open across his knee, and did not draw anything at all.

The page was for the forty-one. The first name he had in his hand. He took Ada Tull's scruple out of his right pocket and closed his bare fingers round it, and it warmed to him at once, the way it had in the Sluice three seasons ago — flour, and a burnt pan, and a doorway full of light — thirty seconds of a woman he had never met, carried about in a boy's coat for half a year like a pebble off a beach.

"Ada Tull," he read. "Of Basin Row, Cold Gantry."

His voice was not steady and he did not wait for it to be.

"There's flour on her hands to the wrist. The pan's gone black at the bottom and she's cross about it, but not properly cross, because it's only a pan. There's a red cloth folded on the sill. There's a doorway with the light of the yard beyond it, and she's calling a child in, and she says the name twice — and the second time it's got the small rising bend at the end that means now."

Nothing struck. Nothing thundered. A seam near the great flank went dark, the way a coal goes out, and along the whole body the light dropped by a fraction so small you had to be watching for it.

The pressure had gone down by exactly one life.

The stone in his hand cooled and went ordinary, clear and dull as a bit of bottle glass off the towpath, because there was nothing in it any more. It had gone home. Down in the town, on Basin Row, a woman of sixty stopped in her own doorway with her hands in the flour and stood quite still while her kitchen came back to her — the black pan, the light through the door, her daughter's name called across the yard and laughed at halfway through. Ada Tull sat down on her own step with her floury hands over her face. She had never known there was anything gone. She had only known that she could not make bread the way she used to, and had blamed the flour for eleven years.

Behind him Hew Marrable sat down on the gallery floor with the lamp still in his hand and wept without any noise at all, because in thirty years nobody with a voice had ever once been let read her.

Then he stopped, all at once, the way a man stops when something arrives, and got up onto his knees.

"It hasn't closed," he said. "Lad. Look at your seam. It's gone dark and it's standing open."

He held his hand flat in the air an inch off the hide, not touching, the way he would have done with a horse.

"Thirty years I've packed her seams shut, and every seam I packed put the pressure somewhere else, and I called that husbandry. That's all a rupture is. That's all it ever was — pressure with nowhere to be. She isn't opening because she's full, she's opening because she's *shut*." His voice went out from under him and he dragged it back. "You've cut a tap in her. One name wide. She can bleed in order now, and a thing that bleeds in order can't burst."

"How long does that hold her?"

"As long as you keep on," Hew said. "And not one hour past that."

A yard along the great flank a seam that had been dull came up bright, the way a lamp is turned up in the next room, with something in it waiting to be said.

"They're not beasts," Phillip said, to the room, to the school over his head. "They're people, filed."

Then he read the second name.

They came for him a little before eleven that morning, and the school's one move was the one it had always had, which was paperwork with legs. Four Auditors down the Sump stair in good coats, unhurried, wiping the wet off their spectacles, with canvas rolls of sealing chalk under their arms — the fat grey sticks that are used for closing a gallery, which is not a lock and works far better than one, because a sealed doorway stops being a doorway and forgets how to be walked through. It is an expensive reckoning. An Auditor's spending is unlimited by charter. That is why Auditors are what you send.

Hew went and stood in the gallery mouth, and was taken out of it, politely, by two men who had been his students, one of them holding his elbow the whole way and saying sorry, sir, in a low voice, over and over, like a boy at a funeral.

Then Nessa Bellhook came down the stair behind them.

She did not make a speech. She put her back against the doorframe and her arms out along it and said the only thing in the world that could have worked, which was not brave and was not even loud. It was a procedure.

"Gallery Nine is under audit. You can't seal a gallery that's under audit — Bursar's own standing order, nineteen, subsection four. I'm the assistant on the audit. I opened it at eight this morning and I haven't signed it off."

She had opened it at eight that morning. She had gone into the office in the dark and written it into the register herself and dated it, which took her about ninety seconds and cost her a career.

One of the Auditors went back up for a ruling. Rulings take time, in a school where the clerks have begun not to come in. By the time anything came back down there were sixty people from Cold Gantry on the Sump stair in their wet coats, sitting on the steps in the dry warm dark, waiting for their names, and you cannot seal a gallery with sixty ratepayers in the way of it.

Nessa did not look at Phillip while she did any of it, and she did not take the armband off, not that day. She was still going to need it.

It took the rest of that day and did not stop after. It could not be done quietly, which was the whole cost of it: a life goes back to whoever lost it, wherever they are standing, in front of whoever is standing with them. By noon a wool factor in Ilmarch's finest street had sat down on his own stairs with everything he had paid the Spendhouse to take. The Quietage's clients got their griefs back and their crimes came with them, in company, and there is no discreet way to hand a man an afternoon he bought his way out of. Within a fortnight the Ledger Houses' paper on Dragonhall was worth nothing at all. The school's money ended, and the school with it, slowly, the way a barge takes on water.

Ilsabet Quorn came down on the second morning, stood at the gallery mouth where her four Auditors had failed to get past a girl of fifteen and a line in a register, and did not stop him. She had sent chalk once. She did not send it twice. Phillip glanced up and saw her doing the sum, four hundred against one against the whole ruined arithmetic of eighty years — and saw her arrive at the bottom of it and stand there.

"I cannot feel the answer," she said. It was the only time in her life she repeated herself. "I know it. I cannot feel it. I have not been able to for a very long while, and I did not notice until now."

Nessa came down on the fourth day and did not go back up. She took the assistant's armband off on the stair and left it folded on the step, where it stayed for a week and then was gone, and nobody ever asked who took it. She did not explain herself. She took the lamp out of Hew's hands and held it steady over the page for eleven hours, which cost her the post and her family's dry roof and every plan she had ever made, and which was the only apology available to her.

"Read on, Phillip," she said.

On the sixth night she nearly got away from him.

He was two clauses into a bargeman named Sowerby — a coil of new rope, a dog asleep on a cabin roof, a girl standing at a lock gate in the rain with her hood down — when the whole gallery came up bright as a forge yard. A seam over his head opened wider than a read seam has any business opening and went on opening, a hand's width, two, silt glaring out of it like sun off water, and the air went so dry so fast that his tongue stuck to the roof of his mouth. That was the Blank coming up out of her the way a wave comes up a slipway, and it was ten feet above his head, and on the steps behind him sixty people from Cold Gantry stood up all together without a sound.

"Don't stop," Hew said, from the floor, absolutely level. "Whatever else you do, don't stop. And don't you dare go faster."

Because stopping shuts the tap. And hurrying is worse than stopping, because a blurred name buys nothing anywhere in Ilmarch, and a name half said is a seam half opened.

So he went slower. He said the dog's ears, one up and one folded over. He said the smell of wet rope, which is not like dry rope. He said the exact tin rattle the lock gate made under the girl's hand, and how she was not pretty and knew it and did not care that morning. He was so frightened that his voice came out flat and careful, like a boy reading a lesson, and it turned out that flat and careful is precisely what the work wants.

The seam did not close. Nothing he read ever closed. But it stopped widening, and hung there, and then it went dark from one end like a wick drowning, and the gallery went back down to its ordinary bread-oven dark, and Nessa's hands on the lamp were shaking so hard that Hew reached up and took hold of her wrist and steadied it for her without a word.

After that he read six hours on and two off, all night and every night, and never once let the reading fall behind her. Twice more that winter she got ahead of him, and twice more the answer was the same one: slower, and truer.

On the ninth day, up out of the dark, one noun came back where it belonged. Odd Pell was sitting on the steps with his hands in his lap when a voice that was not his own said Odalric into the gallery, and he answered to it. Not all of him came back. Enough did to know his own name when it was handed up to him out of the dark, and to say it afterwards without stopping in the middle.

The reading went on into the winter. People came up from Cold Gantry and sat on the Sump steps in their coats and waited for their names, and the town organised it themselves in the end, because the Hall had no clerks left to do it.

With one line left unread on the page, on a night when the lamp was low, Ninefold gave him something he had not asked for.

It came as a smell first, then whole and entire: a morning after a signing, eleven years old, his father sitting out on the lock beam in the wet with a folded paper in both hands, crying, not making any sound about it. The gift he had offered freely and kept and then given away. It came back the way nothing bought ever does.

Phillip held it, and found that he could. It hurt exactly as much as it always had, and he could carry it, and knowing that was the last thing he needed.

He had come to his own number weeks before that. Thirty-three, in its place, in sequence, with the date beside it in somebody's tidy hand and eight more entries still under it. He had gone straight past it that night and read thirty-four instead, and finished the eight, and come back up the page, and gone past it again, and again, and again, until there was nobody left above it and nobody left below it and the paper had gone soft as cloth at the folds and thirty-three sat alone in the middle of it like a stone in a cleared field.

Then he read the name he had been turning past for a month.

"Tobin Vane. Lockkeeper. Gantry Lock Four."

Four years went back, entire, into a man standing at the gallery mouth in his work coat with his cap in his hands, because he had walked up the valley without being sent for and nobody had the heart to stop him. They went back in order, which is the only survivable way: a boy at one, at two, at three, the kitchen, the boots left out to dry, the yard, the small voice in the doorway. And on the end of it, in its proper place, the afternoon he sat at a table in the Spendhouse and signed his son away for a future, because the alternative was the water.

And the doorway came back green. He had sold that green at Michaelmas to be magnificent, and it came home to him now — wet paint, a warped bottom rail, his father filling the frame of it — at the exact moment his father was finding him.

Tobin Vane looked at his son and found something there. All of it.

"Phillip," he said.

They would both have to live with it. That was the point.

Months on, the Sump floor at Gallery Nine looks like this. One lamp, turned low, because the seams give a little light of their own now and there is no money for oil. A page, gone soft as cloth at the folds. A boy sitting cross-legged on warm stone with a coat over his knees, reading aloud, slowly, the way you read to somebody falling asleep.

On the steps behind him, people from Cold Gantry, waiting for their names. Somebody has brought a flask. Somebody's child has fallen asleep against the rail. Three steps up sits Ada Tull with a basket and a cloth over it, because she brings bread down on the nights she is able, and on a string round her neck she wears a little dull lens of glass with nothing whatever left inside it, which he put into her hand himself and which she would not be parted from. The Quietage is finished and Dragonhall is finished and the Counting Bell has not been rung since autumn, and Phillip Vane owns nothing, holds a place nowhere, and has not spent a single coin.

He turns the page.

There are eight more galleries under Barrow Sill, all of them full, none of them read. The next name on the list is not from Cold Gantry at all.

He clears his throat, and begins.
