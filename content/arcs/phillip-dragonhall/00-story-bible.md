# STORY BIBLE — *The Ledger of Ninefold*

**Arc slug:** `phillip-dragonhall`
**Hero:** Phillip, age 15
**Genre:** High fantasy (canal-and-ledger age)
**Rating ceiling:** PG-13 — real stakes, real dread, moral weight. No gore, no sexual content, no profanity, no cruelty played for laughs, no self-harm.
**Format:** 7 sessions × ~2000 words (12–14 min narrated). One continuous arc.

---

## 1. LOGLINE

At a school that teaches children to pay for magic with pieces of their own memory,
fifteen-year-old Phillip Vane sets out to buy back the four years his father sold —
and discovers that the dragons sleeping under the mountain are not monsters guarding
treasure, but archives made entirely of what the school has taken from ordinary
people, that his own place at Dragonhall was purchased with his own childhood, and
that the oldest dragon has grown a self and wants, more than anything, to be read
aloud.

---

## 2. THE WORLD

**Era.** The ninth decade of the Charter. A wet, industrious age of canals and coal,
copperplate handwriting and iron-gall ink, gaslight just arriving in the good streets
and not yet anywhere else. Banking is the great power. There are no guns. There are
barges, ledgers, spectacles, actuaries, and paper — an ocean of paper.

**Ilmarch** is a narrow rain-country of chalk downs and limestone shelves, stitched
together by the canal system that carries coal, lime, and correspondence. Everything
in Ilmarch is measured, invoiced, and written down twice.

**Threnmoor Valley** cuts north into the mountain called **Barrow Sill**. At the head
of the valley, half-built-into the rock, stands **Dragonhall**. At the foot of it,
where the canal terminates in a black basin, sits the town of **Cold Gantry** — four
hundred houses, six chapels, one bell foundry, and the locks.

**What it smells like.** Wet slate. Coal smoke that settles in the valley on still
nights and makes the lamps look furry. Canal water — cold, green, faintly metallic.
Tallow. Wet wool drying too close to a stove. Inside the school: chalk dust, iron-gall
ink (sharp, like a coin held too long in a hand), beeswax, and under everything, from
the deep floors, a warm dry smell like the inside of a bread oven that has gone out.
Students call it *the Sill breath.* It is the dragons.

**What it sounds like.** Rain on slate, which is the true weather of Ilmarch. Barge
horns at the locks, two long and one short. Nibs — hundreds of nibs — on the Tally
Floor, a sound like sleet. The **Counting Bell**, rung at each hour, one stroke per
hundred coins spent in the school that day; students learn to judge a day's weight
before they see anyone's face. And on bad nights, from far below, a sound like a
great slow book being turned.

**Texture of daily life.** Children of merchant houses arrive with trunks and
tutors. Children like Phillip arrive with a bond number. Everyone wears grey wool and
fingerless gloves because the Hall is cold everywhere except above the Sumps. Meals
are oats, salt pork, canal eel, and stewed apple. Letters home are read by the Bursar's
office "for the school's protection." Nobody in Cold Gantry says the word *dragon*
out loud after dark; they say **the old girls**, or nothing.

---

## 3. THE MAGIC SYSTEM — RECKONING

Magic in Ilmarch is called **Reckoning**. It is bookkeeping. It does not create; it
moves debts around. A spell is called **a reckoning**. To work one, you must **spend**.

**The coin.** The price of every reckoning is a *specific* memory of your own — not a
fact, a **particular**. The exact green of a door. The sound of your mother saying your
name across a yard. The weight of a dog's head on your knee. This is **a coin**.

**How a spell is cast.** You draw the tally-sigil, and you *say the coin out loud* —
you narrate the memory in full, precise detail, and on your last word it goes. You
keep the fact; you lose the particulars. Forever after you know you had a dog. You
cannot picture the dog.

> *Craft note for session writers: every spell in this arc is a spoken monologue that
> ends in a loss. Never skip the saying. The saying is the scene.*

**The rules.**

1. **Only your own coins.** You cannot spend another person's memory. (The school can
   *buy* one — see §5 — but the seller must say it themselves.)
2. **Vagueness is worthless.** A blurred memory buys nothing. The clearer and more
   beloved it is, the more it purchases. This is the whole cruelty of the system: the
   best things you have are the strongest currency.
3. **Price scales with load-bearing weight.** A candle-flame costs a rainy Tuesday. A
   locked door costs a birthday. Stopping a falling beam costs a face.
4. **No coin is spent twice, and none comes back.** Diaries, portraits, and witnesses
   restore the *fact* only. Reading your own account of a memory you sold feels like
   reading a stranger's letter about you.
5. **Shared coins.** Two people who witnessed the same thing may pay it together, and
   each loses only half. Afterward neither can recall it alone — only in the other's
   company, and only in pieces. Students do this to survive their exams. It binds them
   for life, which is sometimes love and sometimes a trap.
6. **Hard limits.** Reckoning cannot make matter, cannot close a wound, cannot move
   time, cannot compel a mind, and cannot return the dead. It can move, bind, open,
   close, warm, silence, carry, and hide.
7. **Nothing spent goes away.** This is the rule the school does not teach. A spent
   coin does not vanish — it *silts*. It sinks. It collects. Given enough of it in one
   place, it condenses into a body.

**Overdraft.** A student who spends past their means goes *into arrears*: the losses
start taking things they did not offer. Symptoms are quiet and terrifying — walking
into a room and not knowing which bed is yours; a friend's face going soft at the
edges. Dragonhall treats arrears as a disciplinary matter, not a medical one.

## 4. THE DRAGONS

**What they are.** A dragon is condensed forgetting. Where enough spent memory silts
into one place under enough pressure, it *sinters* — grit, ash, bone-meal, lamp-black,
ledger paper, lost teeth, window glass, hair — pressed into a shape that breathes and
is warm. They are not born and they do not die of age. They **accrue**.

**Body.** No scales; a hide like compacted slate and pressed paper, warm as a loaf,
scored with hairline seams where new matter has packed in. Eyes are the only wet part:
too many of them, arranged in a slow-blinking arc, each one holding a different
reflection than the others. They are enormous, slow, and shockingly quiet. They do not
fly so much as *settle*, like snow going the wrong way.

**Breath.** Not fire. **The Blank.** A dragon's exhalation takes particulars from
whatever it touches. A man who catches a lungful walks out unmarked and cannot say
which hands are his. A wall breathed on stops being *that* wall and becomes merely
wall, and forgets how to hold a roof. It is the most dangerous thing in the world and
it leaves no mark at all.

**Hoard.** Under enormous internal pressure, particulars crystallize into small
lensed stones called **scruples**. A dragon's hoard glitters exactly like coin because
the human eye cannot look at a raw specific and see anything else. Handle a scruple
bare-skinned and you live somebody else's thirty seconds — the smell of their kitchen,
the fright of their fall. Students are strictly forbidden. Students do it constantly.

**Culture.** None, in any human sense. A dragon has no childhood, no kin, no language
of its own. What it has is **contents**. When a dragon "speaks," it is quoting: it
hands you back a smell, a sound, a stranger's sentence in that stranger's voice. Older
accruals can assemble borrowed fragments into something disturbingly close to
conversation — a chorus, every clause in a different person's cadence.

**What they want.** To be **read**. Not fed, not worshipped, not fought. A dragon is a
sealed archive under pressure, and pressure wants out. An unread dragon eventually
**opens** on its own — a rupture that returns everything it holds to everyone who lost
it, all at once, all in a night. The school calls this event **a Restitution** and
considers it a catastrophe. Cold Gantry has an older word for it, from a valley two
ridges over that had one: **the Grieving.**

**Their relationship to the school.** Dragonhall does not fight dragons. Dragonhall
*produces* them and then keeps them asleep. There are nine in the Sumps beneath Barrow
Sill. Internally they are booked as **accruals** — liabilities on the school's ledger,
line-itemed and audited annually.

**The central mystery the arc pays off.** Accrual Nine — **Ninefold** — is different.
It has begun to say *I*. Every other accrual is a heap of a hundred thousand strangers'
losses; Ninefold contains something no other holds: whole lives, sold in complete
sequence, in order, by people who knew what they were doing. And the newest of those
lives are *children's early years*. Somebody at Dragonhall has been buying childhoods
on purpose, and feeding them, in order, to one dragon. **Why** is the engine of the
arc. (Answer: §8.)

## 5. THE SCHOOL — DRAGONHALL

**Full chartered name:** *The Charterhouse of Reckoning at Dragonhall.*
Everyone says **Dragonhall**. Everyone in Cold Gantry says **the Hall**.

**Public face.** Ilmarch's finest academy of Reckoning. Three hundred students. Grey
wool, hard beds, excellent teaching, an unmatched examination record. Its graduates
run the locks, the mines, the assay offices, the mails, and the great banking houses.
The school's public motto, carved over the Tally Floor: **DEBTS ARE PAID BY THOSE WHO
OWE THEM.** Its public duty, for which it draws a Crown stipend, is **wyrm containment**:
Dragonhall keeps the nine old girls asleep so the valley may live.

**Structure.**
- **Tally** — first year. Learns the sigils, spends only trivia.
- **Quire** — second year. *Phillip is a Quire.* First real spending.
- **Reckoner** — third and fourth year. Practical work; permitted below the fourth
  floor.
- **Auditor** — staff. An Auditor's spending is unlimited by charter, which is why old
  Auditors are so calm and so strange.
- **The Chancellor of Account** — the head. Currently vacant in all but name; the
  **Bursar** runs the Hall.

**Who funds it. Three streams, and only one is a secret.**
1. **Tuition**, from merchant and mine-owning families. Respectable. Insufficient.
2. **The Crown stipend** for containment. Political. Shrinking.
3. **The Quietage.** The real money. In the **Spendhouse** — a discreet building with
   its own gate on the Cold Gantry road — Dragonhall will, for a fee, take a memory off
   your hands. Grief. Guilt. The particulars of a crime. The face of a creditor. The
   exact wording of a treaty you would rather not honour. Ilmarch's powerful have used
   this service for eighty years. It is legal, private, and enormously profitable.

**What it hides.** Two things.

*First:* the Quietage's purchases do not go anywhere clean. Every coin taken in the
Spendhouse — and every coin spent by every child on the Tally Floor — runs down the
**Sluice**, a brick throat in the mountain, into the **Sumps**. The school's own
lessons are the feedstock. **Dragonhall is a disposal facility with a curriculum
attached.** The nine accruals downstairs are not a hazard the school inherited; they
are its waste, and it makes more every single day the Counting Bell rings.

*Second, and worse:* the **bond scholarships**. Dragonhall's recruiters find gifted
children in poor houses and offer the parents a bargain — *sell us the child's early
years, and buy the child's future.* The years are paid into the Quietage. The tuition
is paid out of it. The family keeps the child and loses the childhood. There are
forty-one such students in the Hall. Phillip Vane is bond number 33.

## 6. THE CAST

### PHILLIP VANE — 15, the hero

Second-year Quire, bond 33. A lockkeeper's son from Cold Gantry, which makes him one
of eleven boys in the Hall who can splice a rope and the only one who is embarrassed
about it. Sharp, watchful, ferociously good at the one thing the school actually
rewards — willingness to pay.

- **Wants:** to buy back the four years his father sold, and put them back in his
  father's head, so that one man in the world remembers him small.
- **Fears:** that he is forgettable. That when his father looks at him and reaches for
  something and finds nothing there, the nothing is not the school's fault but his own —
  that he was simply the thing worth selling.
- **Flaw:** he overspends. Phillip pays for everything, immediately, with the best coin
  he has, because being needed is the only proof of himself he trusts. He hides his
  arrears. By session four he is walking into rooms and not knowing which bed is his.
  He calls this being generous. It is a debt habit, and it will hollow him out.
- **Arc:** from *paying to be loved* → to *refusing to pay*. He begins believing every
  problem has a price he can meet. He ends holding something that hurts, on purpose,
  unspent, and giving away power instead of memory. His last and largest act in session
  seven costs him **no coin at all** — only his place, his safety, and his friends'
  futures, which is a far harder currency.
- **Speech:** plain, quick, concrete, canal-boy vocabulary — *hitch, slack, draw,
  gantry.* Deflects with a joke exactly one beat too late. Under pressure his sentences
  get shorter and more polite. Verbal tic: he narrates his own coins beautifully,
  helplessly, in far more detail than the reckoning requires, because saying them is the
  last time he will ever have them.
- **Voice:** bright, clear young male, warm at the middle and thin at the edges; steady
  until it isn't.

### NESSA BELLHOOK — 15, Quire, Phillip's best friend

Cold Gantry born, works her family's lock in summer, hands like a rope. Fee-paying by
the skin of her teeth. She sees the Hall exactly as it is and has decided that seeing
it is enough.

- **Wants:** an Auditor's post, a salary, and her family off the water before her
  mother's chest gets worse.
- **Flaw:** complicity. Nessa is brave about everything except her own place. She will
  look away, once, at the worst possible moment, and she will know she did it.
- **Speech:** fast, blunt, consonants clipped, sentences that stop early because the
  point's been made. Canal idiom — *that's a slack rope; don't gantry me.* Calls him
  **Vane**, never Phillip, until it matters.
- **Voice:** quick, warm, slightly hoarse young female; a laugh that arrives before the
  joke lands.

### ODALRIC "ODD" PELL — 16, Reckoner, first in his year

Sponsored by House Aldermew. The finest young reckoner in Ilmarch and very nearly not a
person any more. He has spent so much that his sentences have holes in them.

- **Wants:** to be the best, measured the only way Dragonhall measures anything — by
  what he can afford. He believes a person is a purse.
- **Flaw:** he does not count. Odd cannot conceive of a cost he should decline, and he
  regards Phillip's hesitation as a defect he is generously willing to correct.
- **Speech:** over-formal, citation-happy (*"Marrable, third edition, chapter nine"*),
  and riddled with his tic: he loses nouns mid-sentence and substitutes descriptions —
  *"the thing with the hinges," "the woman who does the soup," "the — the one with my
  name on it."* He never acknowledges the gap. Others must not either.
- **Voice:** reedy, clipped, brittle young male; precise as a metronome, with tiny
  silences in the wrong places.
- **Note for writers:** Odd is not a villain and not comic relief. He is what Phillip
  becomes if nobody stops him. He gets rescued in session six, partially, and it is not
  tidy.

### AUDITOR HEW MARRABLE — 48, Keeper of the Sumps

Thirty years down among the accruals. Smells of hot dust and lamp oil. Wrote the
standard text on dragon husbandry, which Odd quotes at him and which he says was mostly
wrong.

- **Wants:** for one person with authority to say out loud that the old girls are made
  of us — that they are not beasts but *people, filed.*
- **Flaw:** he loves the dragons more than he loves the children. Faced with a choice
  between a student and an accrual, he has already chosen, twice, and both times he
  chose the dragon.
- **Speech:** slow, tactile, farm-and-animal vocabulary — *she's off her feed; mind her
  seams; ease up, ease up.* Long pauses mid-thought. Says **"aye, well"** as a full
  sentence. Calls the dragons **the old girls**; calls Ninefold **her ladyship**.
- **Voice:** gruff, low, kindly older male; gravel and warmth, unhurried.

### BURSAR ILSABET QUORN — 61, who runs Dragonhall

Of the banking Quorns. Came up through the Spendhouse ledgers. Has kept this school
solvent through two Crown reviews and one very quiet Restitution in a neighbouring
valley, and she has done it by paying prices nobody thanks her for.

- **Wants:** the Hall standing, the accruals asleep, and the valley alive, in that
  order, and she can show you the arithmetic.
- **Flaw:** she has spent so much of herself running this place that she can no longer
  *feel* the weight of what she signs. The compassion is intact as a principle and gone
  as a sensation. She is not lying when she says she is doing the merciful thing; she
  simply can't remember what mercy felt like.
- **Speech:** cool, exact, financial. Never raises her voice; never repeats herself.
  Uses the institutional *we.* Answers moral questions with sums. Tic: she prices things
  aloud — *"That would cost the valley four hundred lives, Mr Vane. I have done the
  work. Have you?"*
- **Voice:** dry, cool, precise older female; slate and cold water, courteous to the
  last syllable.

### NINEFOLD — Accrual Nine, roughly 200 years accrued

The oldest thing under Barrow Sill and the only one that has begun to say *I.* Eleven
eyes. Hide like wet paper pressed into stone. Warm as a loaf. It has never harmed
anyone who came to it honestly, which Hew Marrable will tell you and nobody believes.

- **Wants:** to be **read.** Not opened — *read.* Aloud. In order. By a voice.
- **Cost:** to speak with it you must give it a gift, freely, that no one else holds —
  an unspent, whole, private memory, offered rather than paid. It will not take. It only
  accepts.
- **Speech:** in early sessions it does not use words; it answers with returned
  particulars — a smell, a snatch of a lullaby, a stranger's laugh in your ear. From
  session four it assembles sentences out of quotations, each clause in a different
  borrowed cadence, sometimes shifting mid-word. When it says *I*, everything goes quiet.
- **Voice:** layered and many-voiced, low and dry as pages turning; a chorus that never
  quite lands on one speaker.

### TOBIN VANE — 44, lockkeeper, Phillip's father

Keeps Gantry Lock Four. Big hands, bad knee, an unspoken shame he carries like a bag he
won't put down. He remembers *that* Phillip was small. He cannot remember Phillip small.

- **Wants:** to be a good father to a son he can only partly find.
- **Flaw:** shame makes him distant, and he corrects for it with objects — boots, a
  knife, a better coat — instead of words, which makes him look colder than he is.
- **Speech:** plain, short, weather and water. Turns feeling into a task: *"Get that rope
  in, lad."* Calls him **lad**, and only once in seven sessions calls him **Phillip**.
- **Voice:** warm, tired, low male; water-worn, with a rasp at the bottom.

## 7. THE ANTAGONIST FORCE

**The Reckoning economy itself** — the settled, respectable, century-old arrangement by
which the powerful pay to forget, the poor are paid to forget, children are the cheapest
supply, and the residue is bricked up under a mountain and called a hazard.

Its human faces are **Bursar Ilsabet Quorn** and, behind her, the **Ledger Houses**
(chiefly House Quorn and House Aldermew), who hold Dragonhall's paper and audit the
Sumps every spring.

**What it believes it is doing right — and it is not entirely wrong:**

- A Restitution would be a massacre by grief. Two ridges over, a small accrual opened
  and gave four thousand people everything back in one night: every death, every shame,
  every buried thing, all at once, with no time to take it in order. Ninety-one did not
  survive the week. Ilsabet was twenty-three and she was there. Containment is not
  cruelty; containment is the seawall.
- Someone must hold the waste. The spending would happen with or without a school —
  people have always paid to forget. Dragonhall at least keeps it in one place, sedated,
  audited, and away from towns.
- The bond scholarships genuinely lift families out of the water. Phillip *is* being
  educated. Nessa's family *would* drown without the Hall's wages. A poor boy's first
  three years are, in cold arithmetic, worth less than a poor boy's whole future. Ilsabet
  can produce the ledgers.
- And the newest, quietest justification, the one the arc turns on: feeding Ninefold
  *whole, ordered lives* was not greed. It was research. A heap cannot be unpacked. A
  narrative can. Somebody worked out that a dragon fed in sequence might one day be
  **readable** — that Restitution might be made survivable if the archive could be given
  back slowly, one name at a time, instead of bursting. Making Ninefold able to say *I*
  was the most humane act anyone at Dragonhall ever committed, and they paid for it with
  forty-one children's childhoods, and then they got frightened of what they had made and
  bricked up the Sluice gallery and stopped.

The antagonist is not a monster. It is a seawall built by people who were there, funded
by people who were not, maintained on a supply of things taken from children who were
never asked.

## 8. THE ARC PAYOFF

**THE ONE REVELATION.**
Dragons are not the enemy of Dragonhall — they are its product, and Phillip is one of
its ingredients. The four years Tobin Vane sold were not sold to clear a debt, as Phillip
has believed his whole life. They were sold to the Quietage as **the purchase price of
Phillip's admission**: the school buys a poor child's early years and pays the child's
tuition out of them. Those years went down the Sluice into Ninefold, in order, on purpose,
along with forty other children's — and it is precisely those whole, sequenced childhoods
that taught the oldest accrual to say *I.* Phillip's stolen childhood is the reason the
dragon can speak. When Ninefold finally uses a voice of its own, it uses **the voice of
Phillip at three years old**, because that is the only voice it was ever given whole.

**THE ONE CHOICE — Session 7.**
Ninefold is at rupture pressure. It will open within days no matter what anyone does, and
if it bursts, Cold Gantry gets a Grieving. There are two ways to stop that, and Phillip is
the only person both able and permitted to take either.

- **PAY.** Reseal it. This requires one person to spend themselves down to nothing as the
  binding — the school's plan, Ilsabet's arithmetic, and precisely what Phillip's flaw has
  been training him to volunteer for since session one. The Hall stands. The Quietage
  continues. Nessa gets her post. Tobin never learns what he did, which is a kind of mercy.
  And Phillip ends as an emptied thing who kept everyone safe, which the whole book has
  made look like heroism.
- **GIVE IT BACK.** Read the dragon aloud. Open it in the only survivable way — slowly,
  by name, one life at a time, out loud, for as long as it takes. This costs no coin. It
  costs everything else: it takes months, it must be done publicly, it hands the Ledger
  Houses' clients back their crimes and their griefs in front of witnesses, it ends the
  Quietage and therefore the school's finances, it ends Nessa's future and Odd's
  sponsorship, it fills the valley with old sorrow that people had paid good money never
  to feel again, and it returns to Tobin Vane four years that include the afternoon he
  signed his son away.

**He chooses to give it back.** The arc's last image: Phillip on the Sump floor with a
lamp and no sigil drawn, reading names off a dragon's flank in his own unbought voice —
and the last name he reads is his father's. Tobin remembers him at three. All of it. The
kitchen, the boots, the yard, the signing. They both have to live with it. That is the
point: **the story ends by refusing the cheap price and choosing to carry the thing that
hurts.**

**How every session secretly builds to it** *(beat spine — writers may vary texture, not
function):*

1. **The Weight of a Day.** Phillip's first real spend. He pays too much, too willingly,
   and it works — establish the flaw as a *virtue* everyone praises. Ends with the Sill
   breath and a sound from below.
2. **The Sluice.** Phillip and Nessa follow where spent coins go. First scruple touched;
   he lives thirty seconds of a stranger's kitchen. First lie to Nessa about his arrears.
3. **The Old Girls.** Down to the Sumps with Hew Marrable. Accruals seen properly for the
   first time; the Blank demonstrated on a wall. Ninefold notices Phillip and hands him
   back a smell he has never smelled: his own kitchen, aged three.
4. **The Gift.** To speak with Ninefold he must offer, freely, something unspent. The
   dragon speaks in a chorus. Phillip's arrears turn dangerous — he loses which bed is his.
   Odd covers for him, for his own reasons.
5. **The Spendhouse.** The Quietage exposed. Phillip finds bond 33 in the ledger and reads
   what his father actually signed. Ilsabet does not deny a word of it; she shows him the
   arithmetic, and it is good arithmetic.
6. **Arrears.** Ninefold begins to rupture. Odd overspends catastrophically to help and is
   partly lost; Nessa looks away at the worst moment and knows it; Hew chooses the dragon
   over a child, again. Phillip goes home to Cold Gantry and cannot tell his father why.
7. **The Reading.** The choice, above.

## 9. CONTINUITY LEDGER

*Fixed facts. Later writers must not contradict these.*

**Names & spellings (exact).**
Phillip Vane · Tobin Vane · Nessa Bellhook · Odalric "Odd" Pell · Auditor Hew Marrable ·
Bursar Ilsabet Quorn · Ninefold (formally Accrual Nine).
Phillip is always spelled with two L's. "Odd" is a nickname, never "Odds." Nessa is never
"Vanessa." Marrable, not Marable.

**Places (exact).**
Ilmarch (country) · Threnmoor Valley · Barrow Sill (the mountain) · Dragonhall, formally
*The Charterhouse of Reckoning at Dragonhall* · Cold Gantry (the town) · Gantry Lock Four
(Tobin's lock) · the Sumps (dragon galleries) · the Sluice (the brick throat carrying spent
coin down) · the Spendhouse (Quietage premises, own gate on the Cold Gantry road) · the
Tally Floor (exam hall) · the Ledger Houses: House Quorn, House Aldermew.

**Terminology (use these words; do not invent synonyms).**
*Reckoning* (the art) · *a reckoning* (a spell) · *to spend* · *a coin* (a spent memory) ·
*a particular* (the detail lost) · *the tally-sigil* · *arrears* (overspending damage) ·
*a scruple* (crystallized specific, hoard-stone) · *the Blank* (dragon breath) · *accrual*
(school's internal word for a dragon) · *wyrm* / *the old girls* (common words) · *to open*
/ *a Restitution* (dragon rupture) · *the Grieving* (folk word for the one that happened) ·
*the Quietage* (memory-purchase service) · *the Counting Bell.*
Student ranks in order: **Tally → Quire → Reckoner → Auditor.** Phillip and Nessa are
Quires; Odd is a Reckoner.

**Hard mechanics that must never bend.**
- Spells are cast by speaking the coin aloud in detail; it is gone on the last word.
- You keep the fact, lose the particulars.
- You may spend only your own memories. Nothing spent ever comes back by ordinary means.
- Writing, portraits, and witnesses restore facts, never coins.
- Reckoning cannot create matter, heal a wound, compel a mind, move time, or raise the dead.
- Everything spent silts downward. Dragons accrue from silt. No exceptions.
- Ninefold accepts only freely offered gifts; it never takes.
- Dragon breath is the Blank. **There is no fire anywhere in this arc.**

**Who knows what at the start of Session 1.**
- **Phillip** knows: his father sold four years; he was told it was to clear a debt; he has
  a bond number. He does NOT know what the Sluice is, where spent coin goes, that dragons
  are made of it, that there are forty-one bond students, or the true purchase.
- **Nessa** knows the Hall is unfair and has decided not to look. Knows nothing of the
  Spendhouse's function.
- **Odd** knows the Sluice exists and considers it plumbing. He does not know he is in
  arrears.
- **Hew Marrable** knows everything about the dragons' nature and has said so, in print,
  for thirty years; nobody with authority has ever acted on it. He does not know about the
  bond-scholarship program's deliberate sequencing.
- **Ilsabet Quorn** knows all of it, including the research, and authorised it.
- **Tobin Vane** knows he signed something and cannot remember his son small. He has never
  told Phillip the reason.
- **The reader/listener** should suspect §8's revelation by session 5 and be certain by
  session 6. The pleasure of session 7 is the *choice*, not the surprise.

**PG-13 guardrails for all seven sessions.**
Dread is carried by *loss and implication*, never by injury. Permitted: real death
offscreen or reported (the Grieving's ninety-one), genuine physical peril, moral failure by
sympathetic characters, adult betrayal of children, grief. Forbidden: gore, wounds described
in detail, sexual content, profanity, cruelty presented as fun, any depiction or discussion
of self-harm. Phillip's overspending must be written as **debt and compulsion** — a habit of
paying, cheered on by adults who profit — never as self-injury, and it is named and treated
as harm by the story itself. Every session ends on a held breath or a quiet ache, not a
scream. This is read aloud at bedtime; land each ending soft, even when it lands hard.
